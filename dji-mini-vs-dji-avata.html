DJI Mini vs DJI Avata

The DJI Mini 4 Pro and DJI Avata 2 are both made by the same company, cost roughly the same in New Zealand, and do almost completely different jobs. Here's how to tell which one you actually need.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026
How we picked these: We compared official DJI specifications with current New Zealand retail pricing from DJI-authorised NZ resellers, cross-checked against independent hands-on reviews, rather than relying on US list prices.

Two drones, two completely different flying experiences

It's easy to assume "DJI drone under $2,000" is one shopping category, but the Mini 4 Pro and the Avata 2 solve different problems. The Mini 4 Pro is a traditional camera drone: point the controller, watch a screen, get cinematic aerial shots. The Avata 2 is an FPV (first-person view) drone: you strap on goggles, see through the drone's own camera in real time, and fly it more like a fast, low, immersive video game than a photography tool.

Weight, portability and NZ flying rules

The Mini 4 Pro weighs 249g, deliberately kept under the 250g line that matters for airspace rules in a lot of countries. New Zealand's Civil Aviation Authority doesn't currently run a mandatory drone registration scheme (unlike the US), but CAA has signalled that a registration and basic safety test for drones over 250g is coming, so staying under that threshold is still a sensible hedge. The Avata 2 weighs 377g and, combined with its Goggles 3 headset and motion controller, is a noticeably bulkier travel kit.

One NZ-specific wrinkle: CAA's Part 101 rules require you to keep the aircraft in unaided visual line of sight at all times. Because Avata pilots fly wearing goggles rather than watching the drone directly, you legally need a second person acting as a visual observer whenever you fly FPV in open airspace. That's an extra logistics step the Mini 4 Pro simply doesn't need.

Camera and footage quality

For photography and general aerial footage, the Mini 4 Pro wins outright. It shoots stills up to 8064×6048 and has a tighter 82.1° field of view better suited to landscapes and real estate-style shots, plus proper obstacle avoidance in all directions.

The Avata 2's camera is built for immersion, not resolution — a 155° ultra-wide FOV that makes flying through gaps and around trees feel visceral, with stills topping out around 4000×2256 (12MP) and a higher ISO ceiling (up to 25,600) for low-light FPV flying. It's genuinely a different kind of camera doing a different kind of job.

Flight time and safety

The Mini 4 Pro's official flight time is up to 34 minutes on the standard Intelligent Flight Battery, or around 45 minutes with the larger Intelligent Flight Battery Plus. The Avata 2 is rated at roughly 23 minutes per battery — expected, given FPV flying is faster and more battery-hungry, and the airframe carries proper propeller guards for close-proximity flying instead.

Obstacle sensing is the other big safety gap: the Mini 4 Pro has omnidirectional sensors, while the Avata 2 only senses obstacles below it. That's a deliberate trade-off for an FPV drone designed to be flown skilfully through tight spaces rather than to stop itself automatically.

Price in New Zealand

At NZ retailers, the DJI Mini 4 Pro (with the DJI RC 2 controller) typically starts from around $1,300, rising to roughly $2,400 for the Fly More Combo with extra batteries and a carry case. The DJI Avata 2 starts from around $1,240 for the Fly Smart Combo (single battery, goggles and motion controller included), climbing to roughly $1,700 for the Fly More Combo. Prices move regularly between PB Tech, JB Hi-Fi, Ferntech and Drone Depot, so it's worth checking current listings before buying.

Who should buy the Mini 4 Pro

Anyone who wants aerial photos and video for travel, real estate, farms, or general content creation, and wants a drone that's genuinely easy to fly on the first try. The lighter weight, longer flight time and all-round obstacle sensing make it the far more forgiving machine, and the image quality is simply better for anything you'd want to actually post or sell.

Who should buy the Avata 2

Anyone chasing the specific thrill of FPV flying — swooping through forest gaps, following a mountain biker down a track, or filming action sports from angles a normal drone can't reach. It has a steeper learning curve, shorter flight times and needs a spotter under NZ rules, but nothing else at this price replicates that first-person flying feeling.
