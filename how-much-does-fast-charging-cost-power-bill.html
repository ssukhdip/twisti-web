Does Fast Charging Cost More on Your Power Bill?

Fast chargers pull noticeably more power in the moment than a basic charger. Whether that shows up on your power bill is a different question with a genuinely small answer.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026
Short answer up front: No, not meaningfully. The total energy needed to fill a battery is set by the battery's capacity, not how fast you charge it — fast charging finishes sooner rather than using more total electricity. There's a very small efficiency difference, and it doesn't move the needle on a home power bill.

Why charging speed and total energy use are different things

A phone battery holds a fixed amount of energy, typically measured in watt-hours — for context, a modern smartphone battery holds somewhere around 15-20 watt-hours. Charging it fully, whether over 30 minutes with a 45W fast charger or 2+ hours with a basic 5W charger, requires filling that same fixed capacity. Faster charging delivers more watts per second, so it finishes sooner, but the total watt-hours consumed to go from empty to full is close to identical either way.

The actual NZ dollar figure for phone charging

Charging a typical smartphone from empty to full uses roughly 0.01–0.02 kWh. At an NZ average electricity price of roughly 39–42 cents per kWh (MBIE figures cited across NZ power price trackers in 2026, with regional variation from around 35c in the cheapest areas to over 50c in the most expensive), a full phone charge costs a fraction of a cent — realistically under one cent per charge. Charging your phone every single day for a full year lands at roughly NZ$1–3 total, regardless of whether you use fast charging or not.

Where a small real difference does exist

Fast charging does produce more heat, and some of that lost energy is genuine waste rather than useful charge — this is a real, measurable inefficiency, just a very small one in absolute terms. It's more relevant to battery longevity (heat accelerates battery wear over years of daily fast charging) than to your electricity bill, where the difference is well below what you'd notice in even a detailed itemised power bill.

What about laptops?

Laptops draw more power than phones — a fast USB-C laptop charger might run at 65-100W compared to a phone's 20-45W — but the same logic applies: a laptop battery is a fixed capacity (commonly 50-99 watt-hours), and a full charge costs roughly 2-6 cents at NZ power prices regardless of charger wattage, since you're paying for the battery's capacity, not the charger's speed rating.

Where power bills actually move

If you're looking for genuine savings on a NZ power bill, device charging is close to the bottom of the list — heating, hot water cylinders, and older appliances dominate a typical household bill by a wide margin. A fast charger on your phone or laptop is not a place worth optimising for cost; buy for charging speed and convenience without worrying about the electricity impact.
