Best TV Under $1000 (NZ, 2026)

A genuinely good 4K TV under $1000 is easy to find in NZ right now - as long as you know which specs are real and which are just numbers on a sticker.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

What actually matters at this price

Under $1000 buys you a genuinely good 4K TV in 2026 — the trick is knowing which spec sheet numbers are real improvements and which are marketing noise. Here's the short version before we get into named picks.

Panel type: almost everything in this bracket is LED or entry QLED. QLED just means a quantum-dot film sits over the backlight to punch up colour and brightness — it's a genuine (if modest) step up from plain LED, and worth having if the price gap is small. True OLED does not exist under $1000 in NZ right now; if a listing says "OLED" at this price, check it's not a smaller/older clearance size, because the panel cost alone usually rules it out.

Resolution: 4K (3840x2160) is standard on anything 50 inches or bigger these days, so this isn't really a decision point anymore. Don't pay extra for "8K" — there's no 8K content to watch and no reason to want it in this budget bracket.

Refresh rate: 60Hz is fine for normal viewing and most streaming. Some sets in this range advertise "120Hz" or a "Motion Rate/PMI" number in the hundreds — read the fine print. Native 120Hz panels are rare below $1000; a lot of what's advertised is software-interpolated smoothing, which can make movies look artificially soft ("soap opera effect"). If you're not gaming on a PS5/Xbox Series X at 120fps, this spec barely matters.

HDR: look for HDR10 support as a baseline; Dolby Vision is a bonus, not a dealbreaker, at this price. HDR only really shines on TVs bright enough to make it pop, and budget panels vary a lot on peak brightness — reviews matter more than the HDR logo on the box.

Smart TV platform: Google TV, VIDAA (Hisense) and TCL's own Google TV builds are all fine day to day. What matters more in practice is how snappy the interface is and whether it gets updates — cheaper, older-generation sets can feel laggy navigating menus even if the picture itself is decent.

Sound: budget TVs almost universally have thin, small speakers because there's no room inside a slim panel for decent drivers. Set your expectations here rather than being surprised later — see the note on soundbars below.

Our picks under $1000

TCL C6K Mini-LED (55 inch, roughly $900 to $1000)

TCL's mini-LED tech is the standout story under $1000 right now — smaller, more numerous backlight zones than old-school LED means noticeably deeper blacks and better contrast in dark scenes, which is normally a feature reserved for pricier TVs. It also runs Google TV, which most people find intuitive. Best for someone who watches a lot in the evening and wants picture quality that punches above the price, and doesn't mind a smart platform that occasionally nags with recommendations.

TCL P7K QLED (55 inch, around $700 to $850)

A step below the C6K on backlight tech but still QLED colour, still 4K, still Google TV. This is the "just want a solid telly, don't care about the details" option — a sensible size for a lounge, easy to set up, and priced to leave room in the budget for a soundbar. Suits anyone replacing an ageing TV without wanting to think too hard about it.

Hisense 55 inch 4K QLED / U-series entry model (roughly $650 to $950 depending on size and sale)

Hisense's entry QLED range covers similar ground to TCL's P7K — 4K QLED, VIDAA smart platform (a bit less slick than Google TV but works fine), and frequently the cheapest genuine QLED option in the size you want. Worth checking if the 65 inch size fits your budget here rather than stepping down to 55 inch on a pricier brand — a bigger screen from Hisense often beats a smaller screen elsewhere for the same money.

LG QNED entry series (55 inch, often $950 to $1000 on sale)

LG's QNED range (their version of quantum-dot LED) occasionally drops into this bracket at 55 inch, particularly during sale periods. LG's webOS platform is polished and quick, and LG remotes are generally well liked. This is the pick for someone who wants a name-brand feel and slightly nicer software experience and is willing to watch for a sale to hit the budget.

Sizing tip: at this price point, going up a size (55 to 65 inch) usually costs less than upgrading picture tech at the same size. If your room can fit it, a bigger screen on a simpler panel is often the better use of $1000 than a smaller screen with fancier features.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Inflated "was" pricing: some listings show a huge saving off an RRP that the TV was never really sold at for long. Compare the actual current price across two or three retailers rather than trusting the percentage-off sticker on its own.
  • Chasing 120Hz you don't need: unless you're gaming competitively on next-gen consoles, a 60Hz panel with good processing will look better day-to-day than a cheap 120Hz panel with mediocre picture quality elsewhere.
  • Refresh rate marketing terms: "Motion Rate 200," "PMI," "Clear Motion Index" and similar numbers are not the same as native refresh rate — they describe software interpolation, not real panel speed. Ignore these numbers when comparing TVs; they're not standardised between brands.
  • Assuming you need a soundbar immediately: plenty of budget TVs sound perfectly acceptable for casual viewing. Live with the built-in speakers for a couple of weeks before deciding a soundbar is essential — you might not need one, and if you do, a basic budget soundbar can be added later without much outlay.
  • Ignoring ports: check you're getting at least 2 to 3 HDMI ports if you'll be plugging in a games console, a streaming box, and a soundbar all at once. Some budget sets skimp here.

None of the TVs above will blow you away next to a $3000 OLED, and that's fine — that's not what this budget is for. What you're buying under $1000 is a genuinely watchable, 4K, smart TV that will do the job well for years, as long as you pick based on real specs rather than the biggest number on the shelf tag.
