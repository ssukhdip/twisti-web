Best Electric Kettle NZ: Which One to Buy

Kettles all boil water — the differences that matter are temperature control, speed, capacity and how long the thing actually lasts. Here's what's worth paying for.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

Variable temperature: not just a gimmick

Variable-temperature kettles let you set the water to specific temperatures — typically 70-100°C in steps. This genuinely matters if you drink green tea (best around 75-80°C, since boiling water scalds the leaves and makes it bitter) or use a pour-over or espresso setup where water slightly under boiling produces a better extraction. If you only ever make instant coffee or black tea, you don't need this feature — full boil is what you want anyway, and a variable-temp kettle is paying extra for a setting you'll never touch.

Boil speed and wattage

NZ kettles typically run 1800-3000W. Higher wattage boils faster, but the real-world difference for a full kettle is usually 30-90 seconds — noticeable if you're impatient, not life-changing. What matters more is that you're not boiling a full 1.7L kettle just to make one cup; most kettles have a minimum fill line, and topping up with only what you need saves real power and time over months of daily use.

Stainless steel vs glass: the honest trade-off

Stainless steel kettles are more durable, don't show fingerprints, and tend to retain heat slightly longer. Glass kettles look good and let you see the water level and limescale buildup clearly, which is actually useful for knowing when to descale — but they show water spots, and cheaper glass kettles are more prone to element or seal failures over a few years. If you're in a hard-water area (parts of NZ, particularly some Canterbury and Wellington supplies), a glass kettle makes limescale visible early, which is a genuine practical upside, not just aesthetics.

Cordless base, auto shut-off, and other basics

Almost every kettle sold now is cordless (sits on a powered base) — this is standard, not a premium feature. Auto shut-off and boil-dry protection are also standard safety features on anything sold at PB Tech, Noel Leeming, JB Hi-Fi or Harvey Norman; you'd have to actively seek out an old-stock model to miss these. Don't pay extra for a kettle that markets these as special features.

  • Capacity: 1.7L suits most households; 1L is fine for one or two people who don't often fill multiple mugs at once.
  • Variable temperature: worth it for green tea or pour-over coffee drinkers, skip it otherwise.
  • Stainless steel: better long-term durability, especially for daily heavy use.
  • Glass: lets you see and manage limescale buildup, good in hard-water areas.
  • Wattage: higher (2400W+) shaves off boil time but isn't essential.
Honest take: for most people, a solid stainless-steel 1.7L kettle from a known brand covers everything you need. Variable temperature is the one upgrade that's genuinely worth paying for — but only if you'll actually use it.
