Best Massage Gun NZ: Which One to Buy

Percussion massage guns range from $40 no-name imports to $600+ Theragun-branded units. The gap in actual usefulness between them is real, but it's not always where the price tag suggests.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

Percussion depth and stall force — what to actually check

Percussion depth (or "amplitude") is how far the head travels with each strike, typically ranging from around 8mm on gentler units to 16mm on deeper-penetration models. A deeper amplitude reaches muscle tissue more effectively but feels more intense — not automatically "better" if you find it uncomfortable or you're using it on more sensitive areas.

Stall force is how much resistance the motor can handle before it stops moving — essentially how hard you can press the gun into a tight muscle before it bogs down. Budget guns often have low stall force, meaning the motor stalls out exactly when you press firmly into a genuinely tight knot, which is the moment you actually want more power, not less. This is one of the most meaningful differences between cheap and mid-range guns, more so than headline speed numbers.

What the marketing numbers actually mean for you

High RPM/speed alone: mostly marketing — a fast but shallow, low-force motor doesn't necessarily feel more effective than a moderate-speed one with proper stall force.

Amplitude + stall force together: the combination that actually determines whether the gun can do meaningful deep tissue work without bogging down under real pressure.

Noise level differences are bigger than you'd think

Cheap massage guns are genuinely loud — loud enough to be awkward using in a shared lounge or while watching TV. Higher-quality brushless motors in mid-range and premium models run noticeably quieter, which matters more than most buyers expect if you plan to use it regularly at home rather than just at the gym. If noise matters to you, check decibel specs or reviews specifically mentioning noise, not just power output.

Genuine recovery use cases vs marketing claims

Massage guns are genuinely useful for general muscle soreness relief, warming up muscle tissue before exercise, and helping with day-to-day tightness from sitting or physical work. What they're not is a cure for actual injuries, chronic pain conditions, or deep structural issues — despite what some product marketing implies. "Breaking up muscle knots" is a common phrase in advertising, but a muscle knot (myofascial trigger point) isn't literally dissolved by percussion — the temporary relief comes from increased blood flow and nerve desensitisation, not physically untying anything.

When to see a professional instead: ongoing or worsening pain, numbness, or pain following an injury should go to a physio or doctor — a massage gun treats general soreness, not diagnosed injuries.

What actually matters when comparing models

  • Battery life: look for at least 2-3 hours of real use per charge if you want to avoid charging it before every session.
  • Attachment heads: a ball head for large muscle groups and a flat or bullet head for targeted areas cover most needs — you don't need a dozen attachments.
  • Weight and grip: a lighter gun is genuinely easier to use on your own back or shoulders without arm fatigue.
  • Adjustable speed settings: useful for varying intensity between sensitive and less sensitive areas.
  • Warranty length: motors in cheaper imported guns can fail after months of use — a longer warranty is a reasonable proxy for build quality confidence.

Where to buy in NZ

JB Hi-Fi, Noel Leeming and Harvey Norman all stock mainstream massage gun brands including Theragun. PB Tech carries a smaller but often cheaper range. Sports and pharmacy retailers occasionally stock budget or mid-range options too — compare stall force and warranty terms rather than just going by brand name or price alone.
