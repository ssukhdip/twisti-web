Dyson vs Roborock

One you push around for a deep clean, one runs itself every day without being asked. Here is which cleaning habit actually fits your home.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

This is not really Dyson vs Roborock, it is stick vs robot

Worth saying clearly before we go further: Dyson does not make a robot vacuum, and Roborock does not make a cordless stick vacuum. So this comparison is really about two different cleaning philosophies wearing two different brand names - a premium cordless stick vacuum you actively push around versus a premium robot vacuum that cleans on a schedule without you lifting a finger. Both categories have gotten dramatically better in the last few years, to the point where the decision is less about which cleans better and more about which cleaning habit actually fits how you live.

Cleaning power: hand-held control vs autonomous consistency

A Dyson stick vacuum (V15 Detect or Gen5detect) gives you direct, on-demand suction of up to 240 to 280 air watts, laser dust detection that shows exactly what you are picking up, and the ability to point it at anything - stairs, upholstery, car interiors, tight corners behind furniture. You are doing the physical work, but nothing is off-limits.

Roborock's flagship robots, like the Saros 10R, use up to 22,000Pa of suction with a retractable LiDAR turret that lets the unit slim down to duck under low furniture, plus a FlexiArm that extends the side brush into corners the chassis cannot reach. The suction unit itself is genuinely powerful, but a robot's real advantage is not peak power, it is that it runs the exact same clean, on a schedule, every single day, without you doing anything. Consistency beats occasional deep-cleaning power for day-to-day mess control, especially with pets.

Dyson stick vacuum - best for on-demand deep cleans

NZ price: roughly $850 (V15 Detect) to $1,500 (Gen5detect).

Goes anywhere, including stairs, cars, and couches, which no robot vacuum can touch. Runtime of 60 to 70 minutes per charge is plenty for a full-house clean. Requires you to actually do the vacuuming - there is no version of this category that cleans itself.

Roborock - best for hands-off daily maintenance

NZ price: roughly $500 (entry Q-series) to $2,000-plus (Saros 10R with full auto-empty/wash dock).

LiDAR-based mapping builds an accurate floor plan of your home so you can set no-go zones and room-specific schedules in the app. Higher-end models self-empty, self-wash their mop pads, and dry themselves, meaning weeks between you touching the machine at all. Cannot climb stairs, gets confused by thick tasseled rugs and heavy cable clutter, and needs a reasonably clear floor plan to work well.

Where a stick vacuum still wins outright

Stairs are the single biggest one - no robot vacuum on the market climbs stairs, full stop, so any multi-level Kiwi home needs a stick vacuum (or a traditional upright) regardless of how good the robot fleet downstairs is. Car interiors, couches, curtains, and the tops of skirting boards are all stick-vacuum territory too. If your home has a lot of area rugs with fringing, or a genuinely cluttered floor (cables, kids toys, shoes left everywhere), a robot vacuum will spend more time getting stuck or avoiding mess than actually cleaning it, and a stick vacuum's flexibility wins by default.

Where a robot vacuum wins outright

Daily pet hair maintenance is the strongest single case for a robot. The difference between manually vacuuming twice a week and having the mess handled every single day, automatically, is a genuinely different quality of life, especially with dogs or cats that shed heavily. Robots also handle mopping now - Roborock's VibraRise sonic scrubbing and dual spinning pad systems on the Qrevo range do a legitimately good job on sealed hard floors, something a Dyson stick vacuum does not do at all (Dyson does not make a wet-mopping attachment for its cordless line).

Ongoing cost and maintenance

Dyson's ongoing costs are close to zero - an occasional filter rinse, eventually a battery replacement years down the line. Roborock's self-empty, self-wash flagship models need replacement dust bags for the auto-empty dock and, less frequently, replacement mop pads and side brushes - a real but modest ongoing cost, generally lower than the equivalent maintenance for a traditional bagged upright vacuum.

Debunking the myth: robot vacuums have not replaced stick vacuums

A common assumption is that once you own a good robot vacuum, you do not need anything else. In practice almost every household that owns a premium robot vacuum still keeps a stick vacuum or upright around, because stairs, cars, and upholstery simply are not something a robot can do. The two categories are complementary, not competing - think of the robot as handling 80 percent of your daily floor maintenance passively, and the stick vacuum covering the last 20 percent the robot physically cannot reach.

Verdict: who should buy which (or both)

  • Buy a Dyson stick vacuum if: you live in a multi-level home, have stairs, cars, or a lot of upholstery to clean, or your floor plan is too cluttered/rug-heavy for a robot to navigate well.
  • Buy a Roborock robot if: you have a mostly single-level, open-plan home with hard floors or low-pile carpet, and you want daily mess control (especially with pets) without lifting a finger.
  • Budget-conscious buyers: a mid-range Roborock (Q-series, around $500 to $800) handles daily maintenance for a fraction of a flagship's price; pair it with a V15 Detect for deep cleans and you have covered both bases for well under what a single top-tier item from either category costs alone.
  • Can only buy one: if your home suits it, most households get more day-to-day satisfaction from the robot. If your home has stairs or heavy rugs, the stick vacuum is the more flexible single purchase.
Worth knowing: a genuinely large number of Kiwi households end up owning both within a year or two of buying either one - they solve different problems, not the same one twice.
