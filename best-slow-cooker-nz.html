Best Slow Cooker NZ: Which One to Buy

Most people buy a slow cooker that's the wrong size for their household and then wonder why every meal is either too much or barely fills it. Sizing it correctly matters more than any feature list.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

Capacity: get this right before anything else

Slow cookers are sized in litres and the right size depends on household headcount, not on getting the biggest one just in case. A 1.5-2.5L slow cooker suits one or two people and fits comfortably in a small kitchen. A 3.5-4.5L is the genuine sweet spot for a family of three to five, big enough for a proper roast or a large batch of casserole with leftovers. Anything above 6L is really aimed at batch cooking, big families, or people who freeze meals regularly — buying one that big for a couple means constantly cooking half-empty and getting uneven results, since most slow cookers need a reasonable fill level to cook properly.

Programmable timers vs manual dials

A basic manual dial slow cooker (low/high/off) is cheap, reliable and honestly all most people need — you set it before work and it runs until you get home. Programmable models add a digital timer that automatically switches to a keep-warm setting once the cook time finishes, which matters if your schedule is unpredictable and you might not be home right when the food's done. That auto keep-warm function is the actual value-add, not the digital display itself — if you're always home at a predictable time, save the money and buy a manual dial model.

Manual dial vs programmable: who needs which

Manual dial: predictable schedule, budget-conscious, want the simplest possible appliance with nothing to go wrong electronically.

Programmable with keep-warm: unpredictable return times, want to set-and-forget without worrying about overcooking.

Multi-function sear-and-slow-cook units: genuinely useful or just marketing?

Combo units that let you sear meat directly in the same pot before switching to slow-cook mode (rather than dirtying a separate frying pan first) are a genuine convenience upgrade, not just a gimmick — browning meat before slow cooking meaningfully improves flavour through the Maillard reaction, and skipping the extra pan to wash is a real time saver. The trade-off is these multi-function units cost more and the non-stick sear surface wears out faster than the ceramic insert of a dedicated slow cooker if you're using it multiple times a week. If you already own a decent frying pan and don't mind the extra step, a dedicated slow cooker will outlast a combo unit and cost less.

Insert material matters: Look for a removable ceramic or stoneware insert rather than a fixed metal pot — it distributes heat more evenly, is dishwasher safe, and lets you serve straight from the insert at the table.

What to check before buying

  • Litre capacity matched to household size — the single biggest factor in whether you'll actually use it well.
  • Removable, dishwasher-safe insert — makes cleanup dramatically easier than a fixed pot.
  • Auto keep-warm function — valuable if your return-home time is unpredictable.
  • Locking lid for transport — useful if you take dishes to potlucks or family gatherings.
  • Stockist range — Harvey Norman, Noel Leeming and Briscoes all carry a wide range of sizes in NZ, worth comparing capacity and price across all three before deciding.

Bottom line

Size the slow cooker to your household first, then decide between manual and programmable based on how predictable your schedule is. A sear-and-slow-cook combo is worth it if you cook from scratch often; otherwise a simple dedicated slow cooker with a ceramic insert will do the job for years.
