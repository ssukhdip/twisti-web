Best Blender NZ: Which One to Buy

Motor wattage on the box tells you almost nothing on its own — blade design and jar shape matter just as much for whether your smoothies actually come out smooth. Here's what genuinely affects performance.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

Motor power isn't the whole story

A blender advertised at 1200W isn't automatically better than one at 900W, because how that power gets to the blades — through gearing, blade angle and jar shape — matters as much as raw wattage. Cheap high-wattage blenders often just spin faster without cutting more effectively, leaving chunks at the bottom while the top blends fine. Established brands like Vitamix, Breville and even mid-range Russell Hobbs models tend to deliver more consistent results per watt than unbranded high-wattage imports, because the blade geometry and jar taper are properly engineered to pull ingredients back down into the blades.

Glass vs plastic vs Tritan jars

Glass jars don't scratch, don't retain smells or stains, and feel more premium, but they're heavier and can crack if dropped. Standard plastic jars are lighter and cheaper but scratch over time and can pick up odours from strong ingredients like garlic or turmeric. Tritan plastic (used in most higher-end blenders now) resists scratching and staining far better than standard plastic while staying lighter than glass — it's the best all-round choice if you can find it, though it comes at a moderate price premium over basic plastic jars.

Full-size jug blender vs personal blender

Jug blender: best for soups, batch smoothies for a family, crushing ice for multiple drinks, anything needing 1L+ capacity.

Personal/single-serve blender: best for one person's daily smoothie, blending and drinking from the same cup, minimal cleanup, but underpowered for soups or ice-heavy drinks in most budget models.

Smoothies vs soups: genuinely different demands

A budget blender can usually manage a basic fruit-and-yoghurt smoothie fine, but making a genuinely smooth hot soup (blending cooked vegetables to a silky consistency, sometimes while still hot) needs a stronger motor and blade design to avoid a grainy result. If soups, nut butters or crushed ice are part of your regular use, don't buy on smoothie performance alone — check reviews specifically mention thicker, tougher blending tasks. If it really is just smoothies each morning, a mid-range or even a personal blender will handle that fine and you don't need to pay for a high-end jug blender's extra power.

Warranty is a real signal: Brands confident in motor durability tend to offer longer warranties — some premium blenders come with 5-7 year motor warranties, while budget models often sit at 1-2 years. That gap is a reasonable proxy for expected lifespan.

What to check before buying

  • Jar material — Tritan or glass over basic plastic if you can stretch the budget.
  • Preset programs — useful shortcuts (smoothie, ice crush, soup) but not essential if you're comfortable manually controlling speed and time.
  • Ease of cleaning — self-clean cycles and dishwasher-safe parts save real time day to day.
  • Noise level — genuinely varies a lot between models; worth checking if you blend early mornings in a shared household.
  • Where to buy — JB Hi-Fi and Harvey Norman carry the widest blender ranges in NZ; compare wattage claims against actual reviews rather than trusting the box alone.

Bottom line

Buy based on blade and jar design plus real-world reviews, not the wattage number alone. A full jug blender suits families and soup-makers; a personal blender suits single daily smoothies where minimal cleanup matters more than raw power.
