Best Gaming Monitor NZ: Which One to Buy

A 240Hz 4K monitor sounds amazing until you realise your graphics card can't push anywhere near that many frames. Here's how to match a monitor to your actual PC, not your budget for the monitor alone.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

Refresh rate vs resolution: you can't max both on a budget

The single biggest mistake buyers make is chasing the highest refresh rate and the highest resolution at the same time without checking what their graphics card can render. A 165Hz 1440p monitor is genuinely more useful to most gamers than a 4K 144Hz one, because a mid-range GPU like an RTX 4060 or 4070 can actually hit 100+ fps at 1440p in most games, whereas it'll struggle to keep a 4K panel fed above 60fps in anything demanding. If your PC has an RTX 4070 Ti or better, or you're on the newer 5070/5080 cards, 4K at high refresh becomes realistic. Below that, 1440p is the sweet spot for 2026.

Panel type: IPS, VA and OLED are not interchangeable

IPS panels give the best colour accuracy and viewing angles and are now fast enough for competitive gaming, which is why most gaming monitors from Noel Leeming, PB Tech and JB Hi-Fi have moved to IPS as default. VA panels offer deeper blacks and better contrast for dark, atmospheric games but suffer from noticeable ghosting/smearing in fast motion — fine for single-player, less fine for shooters. OLED gaming monitors have dropped in price and deliver genuinely superior contrast and response times, but permanent burn-in risk is real if you leave static UI elements (a taskbar, a HUD) on screen for extended periods, so they suit people who game more than they use the PC as a static desktop monitor.

What GPU actually needs what monitor

Entry GPU (RTX 4060 / RX 7600 class): 1080p 144-165Hz or 1440p 100-120Hz is realistic — don't pay extra for 240Hz you'll never see maxed out.

Mid-range GPU (RTX 4070 / 7800 XT class): 1440p 165Hz is the sweet spot; 4K is playable but usually needs upscaling (DLSS/FSR) to hit high frame rates.

High-end GPU (RTX 4080/4090, 5080/5090 class): 4K 144Hz+ or ultrawide 1440p becomes genuinely worth the money.

Ultrawide and curved: cool for immersion, not for competitive play

34-inch ultrawide curved monitors are fantastic for single-player, flight sims and general desktop multitasking — you get more usable screen real estate side to side. But competitive shooter players tend to avoid them because the stretched field of view can distort peripheral vision judgement, and not every game properly supports the 21:9 aspect ratio. If your gaming is mostly story-driven or strategy titles, ultrawide is worth considering; if it's ranked shooters, stick to a flat 16:9 panel.

Response time claims: Manufacturers advertise 1ms response times using the fastest possible pixel transition (grey-to-grey), which rarely reflects real-world performance. Read independent reviews for actual measured response times rather than trusting the spec sheet number alone.

What actually matters when comparing specs

  • VRR support (G-Sync/FreeSync) — eliminates screen tearing and matters more day-to-day than chasing an extra 60Hz.
  • HDR400 vs HDR600/1000 — HDR400 is largely a marketing badge with minimal real brightness gain; HDR600+ actually looks noticeably different.
  • Port selection — check for DisplayPort 1.4 or 2.1 if you want the full refresh rate at your target resolution, not just HDMI.
  • Stand adjustability — height, tilt and swivel adjustment gets overlooked but matters daily for comfort.
  • Where you buy — Harvey Norman and JB Hi-Fi often bundle deals with GPUs or PCs, while PB Tech tends to have the widest standalone monitor range for NZ buyers.

Bottom line

Buy the monitor that matches your GPU's real-world frame rate output, not the highest number on the box. For most NZ gamers in 2026, a 1440p IPS panel at 144-165Hz is the best balance of price, image quality and actual usable performance.
