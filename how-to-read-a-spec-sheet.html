How to read a spec sheet without getting fooled

Manufacturers know most shoppers skim spec sheets for big numbers. Here's how to spot which numbers actually matter, and which are there to look impressive.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

Big numbers aren't the same as meaningful numbers

Spec sheets are written to be scanned, not read carefully, and manufacturers know that a bigger number generally looks better regardless of whether it translates into a better real-world experience. A phone with a 108-megapixel camera doesn't necessarily take better photos than one with 48 megapixels — sensor size, processing software and lens quality all matter more than raw megapixel count once you're past a certain threshold. The same pattern shows up across almost every product category: headline numbers get inflated because they're easy to compare at a glance, even when they're a poor proxy for actual quality.

Category-specific traps

Different product categories have their own favourite misleading metrics. Knowing the common ones for what you're buying saves a lot of second-guessing later.

  • TVs: "Refresh rate" numbers are sometimes inflated through interpolation techniques rather than reflecting a true native refresh rate — check for "native" or "true" refresh rate specifically. Contrast ratio numbers are also frequently marketing figures rather than standardised measurements.
  • Laptops: RAM and storage capacity are easy to compare, but processor generation and type matter far more for actual performance than clock speed alone. A newer, lower-clocked chip regularly outperforms an older, higher-clocked one.
  • Headphones and speakers: Wattage numbers are notoriously inconsistent between brands, often measured under different conditions. A "50W" speaker from one brand isn't necessarily louder or better than a "30W" speaker from another.
  • Appliances: Energy star ratings are standardised and genuinely useful, but capacity numbers (litres, kg) are sometimes measured to the maximum physical limit rather than practical usable capacity.

Headline spec vs real-world spec

A headline spec is the number in bold on the box — megapixels, wattage, refresh rate, RAM. A real-world spec is the number (or the review) that tells you how the product actually performs for the thing you'll use it for. When the two disagree, trust independent reviews and real-world testing over the marketing figure every time.

Questions worth asking instead

Rather than comparing raw numbers across two products, it's more useful to ask what you'll actually notice in daily use:

  • Will I actually perceive the difference between these two specs, or is it below the threshold where it matters?
  • Is this number measured under standardised, comparable conditions, or is it a manufacturer-specific test?
  • What do independent reviews say about real-world performance, separate from the spec sheet?
Caveat: Not every big number is misleading — some genuinely matter (native contrast ratio on TVs, actual battery capacity in mAh, true refresh rate). The goal isn't to distrust every spec, just to check whether a given number is standardised and relevant before letting it drive your decision.

The practical approach

Use the spec sheet to narrow down options, then check independent reviews before making a final call — especially on anything where the spec sheet numbers between two products look suspiciously different despite similar price points. A large gap in a headline number, with a much smaller price gap, is often a sign that the number isn't measuring what you think it is.
