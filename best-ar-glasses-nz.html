Best AR Glasses NZ: Which One to Buy

AR glasses aren't one product category — tethered display glasses that project a giant virtual screen are genuinely different from camera-based AI smart glasses. Here's the distinction that matters.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

Two very different products wearing the same name

"AR glasses" gets used loosely to cover two genuinely different products. The first — camera-based AI smart glasses, like Ray-Ban Meta style devices — are built around cameras, microphones and an AI assistant, with minimal or no visual display. The second — tethered display glasses from brands like XREAL, Rokid and Viture — plug into a phone, laptop or console via USB-C and project a large virtual screen (often equivalent to a 100-200 inch display at a set viewing distance) directly in front of your eyes, using the connected device's screen output rather than their own processing. This article is specifically about the second category, since it's the one genuinely useful for entertainment and productivity right now.

Field of view: the spec that decides whether it's usable

Field of view (FOV) is measured in degrees and determines how much of your vision the virtual screen fills — most consumer tethered glasses sit somewhere between 40 and 60 degrees diagonal. Lower-FOV models feel like watching a screen through a window, with visible edges at the periphery. Higher-FOV models feel more immersive and closer to a genuine giant-screen experience, but usually cost more and can be harder to focus on for people who wear prescription glasses, since the sweet spot for sharp focus narrows as FOV increases.

Comfort for extended wear: the thing most reviews skip

Weight matters more than any spec sheet number once you've worn a pair for over 30 minutes — most tethered AR glasses sit in the 75-100g range, noticeably heavier than regular sunglasses, and nose-bridge pressure becomes the limiting factor for long sessions rather than eye strain. If you wear prescription glasses, check whether the model supports clip-in prescription inserts or if you'll need to use it over your existing glasses, which changes both comfort and fit significantly. Ventilation also matters in NZ's more humid regions — some models fog up during extended indoor use in poorly ventilated rooms.

Realistic use cases vs the hype

The genuinely strong use case for tethered AR glasses right now is travel entertainment — plugging into a laptop, Steam Deck, or phone on a long-haul flight or bus/train trip to get a large private screen without needing an actual TV or monitor, in a form factor that fits in a jacket pocket. Gaming on the go with a Steam Deck or ROG Ally is the other standout case, especially for people who travel a lot. Where the hype outruns the reality is treating these as a replacement for a monitor at a desk (eye strain builds up faster than most marketing admits over multi-hour sessions) or as an everyday-wear AR device for walking around — battery life, the tether, and social awkwardness of wearing them in public all limit that use case today.

  • Travel entertainment (flights, trains, buses): the strongest, most genuine use case right now.
  • Handheld gaming on the go: pairs well with Steam Deck, ROG Ally or a phone.
  • Desk monitor replacement: possible but expect more eye strain over long sessions than a real monitor.
  • Field of view: higher FOV feels more immersive but can be harder to keep in sharp focus.
  • Weight and prescription support: check both before buying if you plan on wearing them for more than short sessions.
Honest take: if you're picturing all-day AR that overlays information on the real world, that's still mostly the camera-based smart glasses category, not tethered display glasses. Tethered AR glasses are, right now, genuinely good at one thing: a private big screen for entertainment and gaming on the move.
