Best Rice Cooker NZ: Which One to Buy

Rice cookers range from $25 basic switch models to $400+ induction units with fuzzy-logic brains. Here's what the price gap actually buys you, and when it's not worth paying for.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

The three tiers, honestly explained

Every rice cooker on the market falls into one of three categories, and the differences between them are real — not just marketing dressing. A basic switch cooker heats until the water boils off, then a thermostat clicks over to "keep warm." That's it. It works fine for everyday white rice and costs very little at PB Tech or The Warehouse.

Fuzzy-logic cookers use a microchip to adjust cooking time and temperature based on how much rice and water is in the pot, and how it's behaving as it cooks. This matters more than people expect — it's the difference between rice that's slightly gluey on the bottom and rice that's evenly cooked top to bottom. Brands like Zojirushi and Tiger (sold through PB Tech and some specialty importers) built their reputation on this.

Induction heating (IH) cookers go a step further, heating the entire inner pot rather than just the base, giving more even and responsive temperature control. This is where you start seeing genuinely noticeable texture improvements for short-grain and sushi rice specifically — less so for long-grain basmati or jasmine, which are more forgiving.

Does the upgrade actually matter for you?

If you cook plain white rice a few times a week as a side dish, a $40-$60 basic cooker from Noel Leeming or Harvey Norman does the job — most people genuinely cannot tell the difference in a blind taste test for everyday jasmine rice. The fuzzy-logic and IH upgrades pay off if you cook rice daily, care about sushi-grade texture, or regularly cook brown rice and mixed grains, which basic cookers handle poorly because they can't adapt timing.

Multi-function models: rice, steam, slow cook

Mid-range cookers ($80-$150) increasingly bundle in steaming baskets, slow-cook settings, and porridge/congee modes. The steaming function is genuinely useful and something people actually use — steam vegetables or fish over the rice while it cooks, one appliance, one bench spot. The slow-cook setting is more hit-and-miss; most rice cooker slow-cook modes don't get hot enough for safe, proper slow cooking of raw meat and are better thought of as a "keep it warm and simmering" mode rather than a real slow cooker replacement.

  • Basic switch cooker ($25-$60): fine for everyday white rice, no learning curve, cheap to replace.
  • Fuzzy-logic ($100-$200): worth it if you eat rice most days or cook a mix of grain types.
  • Induction (IH) ($200-$450): best texture, best for sushi rice, diminishing returns for casual users.
  • Multi-function with steamer basket: genuinely useful add-on, check it's included rather than sold separately.
  • Capacity: a 5.5-cup (uncooked) cooker suits 2-4 people; go 10-cup for larger households or meal prep.

Where to buy in NZ

PB Tech and Noel Leeming carry the widest range from budget to premium fuzzy-logic models. JB Hi-Fi's range skews toward the budget-to-mid tier. Harvey Norman tends to stock a few premium Japanese-brand options alongside budget lines. Because pricing on the same model can vary by $20-$40 between retailers depending on current promotions, it's worth checking before you buy rather than assuming any one store is always cheapest.

Honest take: unless you're cooking rice most days of the week or you specifically care about sushi-grade texture, the $300+ induction cookers are overkill. A $100-ish fuzzy-logic model covers almost everyone's real needs.
