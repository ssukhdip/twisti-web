Tesla Powerwall vs Sungrow vs GivEnergy vs Enphase: NZ Home Batteries

Every brand advertises a headline capacity figure, but usable capacity, installer availability and how the warranty actually treats degradation matter far more to your power bill over ten years. Here's how the main brands sold in NZ actually compare.

Twisti guides · Updated August 2026

The four brands NZ installers actually quote

Walk into a quote process with an NZ solar installer in 2026 and you'll almost always be offered one of four brands: Tesla Powerwall (now Powerwall 3), Sungrow (SBR/SH-RS series), GivEnergy, or Enphase (IQ Battery range). Each has a genuinely different installer footprint in NZ, and that footprint matters more than most spec-sheet comparisons suggest — a battery your local installer doesn't regularly work with means slower fault diagnosis and longer waits for warranty parts.

Installed pricing in NZ (2026, realistic ranges)

  • Tesla Powerwall 3: Around $16,000-19,000 installed for a single unit (13.5kWh usable), reflecting Tesla's premium positioning and integrated inverter.
  • Sungrow SBR (modular): Around $10,000-14,000 installed for roughly 9.6-12.8kWh, often cheaper per kWh than Powerwall and popular with NZ installers because it stacks in 3.2kWh modules — you can start smaller and add capacity later.
  • GivEnergy: Around $11,000-15,000 installed depending on capacity chosen (typically 9.5-13kWh), UK-designed but with a growing NZ distributor and installer base.
  • Enphase IQ Battery 5P: Around $12,000-17,000+ depending on how many 5kWh units are stacked, distinct because it pairs specifically well with Enphase microinverter solar systems (if your panels already use Enphase microinverters, sticking with Enphase batteries avoids inverter compatibility headaches).

Usable capacity vs rated capacity — read the fine print

Tesla Powerwall 3: Rated 13.5kWh, usable is also effectively 13.5kWh — Tesla doesn't reserve much buffer, one of its genuine advantages.

Sungrow SBR: Rated capacity is typically usable capacity minus roughly 5-8%, so a "12.8kWh" system delivers closer to 11.8-12.2kWh usable in practice.

GivEnergy: Similar story — usable is typically 90-95% of the rated figure depending on the specific model and depth-of-discharge setting configured at install.

Enphase IQ Battery 5P: Rated and usable are close (around 4.96kWh usable per 5kWh unit), similar to Tesla's approach.

Installer network strength across NZ

Tesla Powerwall has the most consistent installer training and support in main centres (Auckland, Wellington, Christchurch, Hamilton, Tauranga) but thinner coverage in smaller regional towns. Sungrow has quietly become one of the most-installed brands in NZ because so many solar installers already use Sungrow inverters for the solar side, making a same-brand battery installation simpler and often cheaper on labour. GivEnergy and Enphase both have solid but smaller installer bases — worth confirming your specific installer has completed brand-specific certification before committing, since battery installation quality (particularly around backup-circuit wiring) varies a lot installer to installer regardless of brand.

Warranty length and degradation guarantees

All four brands advertise a 10-year warranty, but the degradation guarantee — how much capacity loss is covered before the warranty triggers a replacement — differs and this is the number that actually matters long-term.

  • Tesla Powerwall 3: 10 years, guarantees retention of at least 70% of original capacity at the 10-year mark.
  • Sungrow SBR: 10 years, typically guarantees around 70-80% retention depending on the exact model and firmware-managed cycling limits.
  • GivEnergy: 10 years, similar 70% retention floor, with some product lines offering extended warranty purchase options.
  • Enphase IQ Battery: 10 years (some documentation cites a 15-year option on certain models), with degradation guarantees in a comparable 70% range.
Concrete tip: Ask your installer for the warranty's exact cycle-count limit, not just the year count — most NZ battery warranties cap at around 37,800-70,000 lifetime kWh throughput depending on brand. If you're running a household that fully cycles the battery daily (charge to 100%, discharge most of it overnight), do the maths: a 13.5kWh battery cycled fully 365 days a year uses roughly 4,900kWh of throughput annually, so a 10-year warranty with a 50,000kWh cap gives you real headroom; a smaller battery cycled harder can approach its cycle cap before the 10 years is up.

Payback period for NZ power prices

With residential power prices in NZ averaging roughly 28-35c/kWh (varying significantly by region and retailer, higher on some fixed-line-charge plans), a well-sized home battery paired with solar typically pays back over 7 to 12 years, depending on how much of your usage happens after sunset and whether your retailer offers a decent solar buyback rate (many NZ buyback rates sit surprisingly low, around 8-12c/kWh, which materially changes the maths in favour of storing your own solar rather than exporting it). Households with high evening usage (dinner, dishwasher, EV charging overnight) get the fastest payback since the battery directly offsets peak-rate grid import rather than just capturing exported solar credit.

Bottom line

If you want the simplest install with the strongest usable-capacity-to-rated ratio and don't mind paying a premium, Tesla Powerwall 3 is the safe default. If you're optimising for cost per usable kWh and your installer already works with Sungrow inverters, Sungrow is usually the better value pick in 2026. GivEnergy and Enphase are both solid choices in specific situations — GivEnergy for flexible capacity stacking, Enphase specifically if your solar already runs Enphase microinverters. Whichever brand, always ask for the exact degradation guarantee and cycle-count cap in writing before signing, not just the headline "10 year warranty" line.
