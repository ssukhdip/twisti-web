MacBook Air vs Surface Laptop

Two of the best thin-and-light laptops you can buy, built on completely different philosophies. Here's which one actually suits you, with real 2026 NZ pricing.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

Two very different ideas of a thin laptop

The MacBook Air (M4, 13.6-inch and 15.3-inch) and the Surface Laptop are the two default answers to 'I just want a nice thin laptop that doesn't die by lunchtime.' They're aimed at the same buyer — students, road warriors, anyone who wants a machine that disappears into a bag — but they get there through completely different hardware and completely different operating systems, and that second part matters more than most spec sheets let on.

Current MacBook Air pricing in NZ starts around $1,699-$1,999 for the 13-inch 16GB/256GB model, climbing past $2,178 for the 15-inch. The current Surface Laptop (running Qualcomm's Snapdragon X Plus or X Elite silicon as a Copilot+ PC, with an Intel version also on shelves for business buyers) starts more expensive, with 16GB/512GB configurations landing around $2,445 from Harvey Norman and other NZ retailers.

Build and design

MacBook Air

Unibody CNC-machined aluminium, 1.24kg for the 13-inch, completely fanless. It's the benchmark other thin-and-lights get measured against, and four years after the redesign it still feels stiffer and more solid in the hand than most Windows competitors at this weight.

Surface Laptop

Also aluminium, also excellent — Microsoft's industrial design team is genuinely Apple's equal here. The 13.8-inch and 15-inch bodies feel dense and premium, and the keyboard deck is arguably the more comfortable typing experience of the two thanks to slightly deeper key travel.

Display

Both use sharp, colour-accurate panels, but they're tuned differently. The MacBook Air's Liquid Retina display hits around 500 nits and has a slightly cooler, more clinical calibration that photo and video editors tend to prefer. The Surface Laptop's PixelSense display runs a 3:2 aspect ratio, which gives you noticeably more vertical space for documents, spreadsheets and web pages — genuinely useful if you spend your day scrolling rather than watching video.

Performance

This is where it gets interesting rather than obvious. Apple's M4 (10-core CPU, 8 or 10-core GPU) is a known quantity: fast, efficient, and it runs every native Mac app without compromise. The Surface Laptop's Snapdragon X Elite/X Plus chips are also genuinely fast for everyday work and sip power doing it — but Windows-on-ARM still has pockets of software, particularly older utilities, niche enterprise tools, and some games, that either run through emulation with a performance hit or don't run at all. If your workflow is entirely web browsers, Office, and mainstream apps, this won't touch you. If you rely on a specific legacy Windows program, check compatibility before you buy — it's the single biggest practical gotcha in this comparison, more than any benchmark number.

Battery life

Both are genuinely excellent, and this used to be Apple's uncontested advantage — it no longer is. The MacBook Air comfortably delivers a real 15-18 hours of mixed use. The Snapdragon-powered Surface Laptop claims similar or slightly longer figures thanks to the ARM chip's efficiency, and in practice both will get an average user through a full working day without hunting for a charger. Neither is the deciding factor anymore; buy on ecosystem and software fit instead.

Ports and repairability

  • MacBook Air: two Thunderbolt/USB4 ports plus MagSafe charging and a headphone jack. No USB-A, no card reader. Internal storage and RAM are soldered and not upgradeable after purchase.
  • Surface Laptop: USB-C/USB4 plus, on most configurations, a USB-A port — a real convenience if you still plug in older peripherals, wired mice, or presentation clickers. Storage is soldered too; neither machine is meant to be opened up and upgraded.
Myth worth killing: 'Windows laptops are cheaper than Macs.' Not this pair. Once you configure a Surface Laptop with 16GB of RAM and 512GB of storage to match a comparable MacBook Air, the Surface is usually the more expensive machine in NZD. The MacBook Air's entry price is genuinely competitive — it's the storage and RAM upgrades on both sides that get expensive fast.

Software ecosystem — the real decision

This is the part that should actually drive your choice, not the spec sheet. macOS gives you tight integration with an iPhone (AirDrop, Handoff, iMessage, universal clipboard) and a mature, stable app catalogue where almost everything just works. Windows gives you far broader compatibility with business software, PC gaming (even with ARM's emulation quirks, x86 Windows laptops remain the safer gaming bet), and easier interoperability with Android phones and Windows-based workplace tooling. If your phone is an iPhone, the MacBook Air's ecosystem pull is strong. If you're in a Windows-managed corporate environment or you occasionally need a specific Windows-only tool, the Surface Laptop removes friction the Mac can't.

Verdict

Buy the MacBook Air if: you own an iPhone, you want the longest track record of battery life and build quality holding up over years, your work is web/Office/creative apps, and you want the lowest entry price for a genuinely premium thin laptop.

Buy the Surface Laptop if: you're in a Windows/Microsoft 365 environment, you want the taller 3:2 screen for productivity, you need a USB-A port without a dongle, or your phone is Android and you want tighter Windows-Android integration via Phone Link.

Our honest take: for most everyday buyers without a specific Windows dependency, the MacBook Air is the safer default purchase — cheaper to enter at, longer resale value historically, and zero software-compatibility roulette. The Surface Laptop earns its higher price in specific, work-driven scenarios rather than as a general recommendation.
