Best Ultrathin Power Bank NZ: Which to Buy

New silicon-carbon battery chemistry is letting power banks get noticeably thinner and lighter without the usual capacity sacrifice. Here's what's actually changed and what still isn't perfect.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

What's actually new with silicon-carbon batteries

Traditional power banks use lithium-ion or lithium-polymer cells, and for years the rule was simple: more capacity meant more bulk, no way around it. Silicon-carbon (sometimes marketed as silicon-anode) battery tech changes the internal chemistry to pack more energy density into a smaller, thinner cell. In practice, this means a 10,000mAh power bank that used to be roughly 15-18mm thick can now come in at closer to 10-12mm — genuinely pocketable in a way older power banks weren't, without the usual step down in battery capacity to achieve it.

The real trade-off: it's not free

Silicon-carbon cells are still a newer, more expensive technology to manufacture, so ultrathin power banks using it carry a price premium over a chunkier standard lithium-polymer bank of the same capacity. There's also a valid long-term question mark on charge cycle longevity compared to well-established lithium-polymer cells, since silicon-carbon is still relatively new to mass consumer devices — early data looks solid, but it hasn't had a decade of real-world testing behind it the way standard lithium-poly has.

Ultrathin vs standard power bank: which to buy

Ultrathin silicon-carbon: best for everyday-carry in a pocket or slim bag, frequent flyers wanting minimal bulk, anyone who currently avoids carrying a power bank because it's too chunky.

Standard lithium-polymer: best for maximum capacity per dollar, home/office backup use where thinness doesn't matter, and slightly more established long-term reliability.

Genuine everyday-carry use cases

Where ultrathin banks make the most sense is exactly what the name suggests — carrying one every single day without noticing it, rather than only packing a power bank for specific trips. A card-thin 10,000mAh bank slipping into a laptop bag pocket or even a jacket pocket removes the excuse of not bringing a power bank that chunkier ones create. If you only need backup charge occasionally — a flight, a festival, a camping trip — the thickness matters far less and you're better off saving money on a standard bank with the same or higher capacity.

Check output, not just capacity: A high mAh rating means nothing if the output wattage is low. For fast-charging a modern phone or topping up a laptop, look for at least 20W USB-C PD output, ideally 30W+ if you want to charge a laptop reasonably from it.

What to check before buying

  • Actual usable capacity — rated mAh is always higher than what reaches your device due to voltage conversion losses, expect roughly 60-70% of the stated figure in real charge delivered.
  • USB-C PD output wattage — determines how fast it actually charges your gear.
  • Pass-through charging — useful if you want to charge the bank and a device simultaneously overnight.
  • Airline carry-on rules — anything over 100Wh needs airline approval and most over 160Wh isn't allowed at all; ultrathin banks are almost always well under this, worth checking the spec sheet regardless.
  • Where to buy — PB Tech and JB Hi-Fi stock the newer silicon-carbon models in NZ; compare against standard banks before paying the premium.

Bottom line

Ultrathin silicon-carbon power banks are a genuine step forward for people who want to carry charge every day without the bulk, and they're worth the premium if that's your use case. If you only need backup power occasionally, a standard lithium-polymer bank gives you more capacity for less money.
