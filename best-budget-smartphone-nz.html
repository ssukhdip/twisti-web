Best Budget Smartphone Under $600 (NZ, 2026)

You don't need to spend flagship money to get a smartphone that takes good photos, lasts all day and won't feel sluggish in a year. Here's what actually separates a good sub-$600 phone from a bad one in NZ right now.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

What actually matters at this price

Under $600 in NZ buys a genuinely capable phone in 2026 — the market has matured enough that "budget" no longer means laggy and short-lived. But the corners that do get cut are consistent across brands, so it pays to know what to check before the price tag.

Chipset and RAM: this is the single biggest factor in how the phone feels three years from now, not just on day one. Look for at least 6-8GB of RAM and a mid-tier chipset from the last one to two generations (Snapdragon 6-series, Dimensity 6000/7000-series, or Google's Tensor on older Pixels). Older or entry-level chipsets are the main reason budget phones start to feel sluggish once a couple of OS updates land.

Software update commitment: this varies enormously and matters more than almost any other spec if you plan to keep the phone a while. Some brands now commit to 3-4 years of OS updates even on budget models; others quietly stop at one or two. Check the manufacturer's official update policy for the specific model, not just the brand's flagship policy.

Battery and charging: a 5000mAh battery is close to standard now and should comfortably get most people through a full day. Charging speed varies a lot — some budget phones still ship with slow chargers in the box, or no charger at all, so factor that into the real cost if you don't already own a compatible one.

Cameras: the main sensor on most sub-$600 phones is now genuinely decent in good light. Where budget phones fall down is low light, and in the extra "filler" lenses (macro, depth) that sound good on a spec sheet but add little real value. A phone with one solid main camera usually beats one with four mediocre ones.

Display: a 90Hz or 120Hz refresh rate is common even at this price now and makes scrolling feel noticeably smoother — worth prioritising over a slightly sharper resolution. Full HD+ resolution is plenty for a phone-sized screen.

Build and durability: plastic frames are standard under $600 and that's fine — check for at least an IP54 rating for basic splash resistance if you can get it, though full IP67/68 is rare at this price.

Our picks under $600

Samsung Galaxy A-series (mid-range A3x/A5x tier)

Samsung's A-series remains the safest all-rounder choice in NZ — widely stocked at JB Hi-Fi, Noel Leeming and PB Tech, with a camera system that punches above its price and Samsung's own long software support commitment on this range. The trade-off is a chipset that's a step behind rivals at the same price, so it suits someone who values a polished, familiar interface (One UI) and camera quality over raw benchmark speed.

Google Pixel A-series

When it launches within budget or drops into this bracket on sale, the Pixel A-series is hard to beat for camera processing and a clean, fast version of Android with strong update commitments. It typically has the weakest battery life of the group here, so it suits someone who charges overnight without fail rather than someone needing to stretch a single charge over two days.

Nothing Phone (budget tier)

Nothing's more affordable models bring a genuinely different design language and a clean near-stock Android build, with hardware that's usually a notch ahead of similarly priced rivals on raw performance. Software support commitments are shorter than Samsung's or Google's, so this suits someone who upgrades every couple of years rather than holding onto a phone for the long haul.

Oppo / OnePlus budget tier

These brands consistently lead on charging speed at this price point — fast enough that a full charge can take well under an hour — and pack a strong chipset for the money. The main watch-out is checking the specific NZ software update commitment before buying, since it varies by model more than with the other brands here.

Secondhand tip: a one or two-year-old flagship bought secondhand or refurbished through a reputable NZ seller often outperforms a brand-new sub-$600 phone on camera and chipset, for similar or less money. It's worth comparing both routes before deciding, especially if you don't need the reassurance of a full new-phone warranty.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Chasing megapixel counts: a 108MP sensor on a budget phone rarely outperforms a well-tuned 50MP one — processing and sensor size matter more than the raw megapixel number.
  • Ignoring the update policy: a phone that stops getting security updates after 18 months is a worse long-term buy than a slightly older model still under active support, even if the spec sheet looks similar.
  • Forgetting the charger: some budget phones ship without one in the box now. If you don't already have a compatible fast charger, add its real cost to your comparison.
  • Comparing RRP instead of real prices: budget phones move on and off sale constantly across NZ retailers — check current pricing across two or three stores rather than trusting a single listing's "was" price.
  • Overbuying storage you won't use: 128GB is plenty for most people who lean on cloud photo backup and streaming rather than local storage. Paying extra for 256GB is only worth it if you know you need it.

A good sub-$600 phone in 2026 will comfortably handle everyday use, take photos you're happy to share, and get you through a full day without hunting for a charger. Picking the right one is less about finding the "best" phone on paper and more about matching the trade-offs above to how you'll actually use it.
