Smart Glasses NZ: What to Know Before Buying

Meta's Ray-Ban glasses (including the new Display model) are the first smart glasses that non-tech-heads are actually buying. Here's what they do well, where they fall short, and whether they suit you.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

What smart glasses actually do

Strip away the marketing and current-generation smart glasses like the Meta Ray-Ban range cover four jobs: taking photos and video from a first-person point of view, playing audio through open-ear speakers built into the arms, answering questions via a built-in AI assistant that can "see" what you're looking at, and — on the newer Display model — showing basic notifications, turn-by-turn directions or a live caption feed on a small display visible in one lens.

None of this replaces a phone. It supplements it. Photos and video sync to a companion app, calls and messages route through your paired phone, and the AI assistant needs an internet connection to do anything useful.

Battery life: the honest picture

Standard Ray-Ban-style smart glasses with camera and audio get you somewhere around 4-6 hours of mixed use — noticeably less if you're recording video regularly, since that's the biggest power draw. The charging case adds several full recharges on top, which is how most people actually get through a day: pop them in the case for ten minutes between uses. The Display model, with its always-available screen, tends to sit at the lower end of that range. If you're expecting all-day wear without touching the case, you'll be disappointed.

Camera glasses vs Display glasses

Camera-only models are lighter, cheaper, and better battery life — good for capturing moments hands-free and quick AI questions. Display models add a heads-up screen for notifications, translation subtitles and navigation, but cost more, weigh more, and drain faster. If you're not sure you need a display, start with a camera-only pair.

Privacy — yours and everyone else's

These are recording devices worn on your face, and that's a real issue, not just a talking point. Most models have an LED that lights up while recording, but it's small and easy to miss from a distance, which is why some venues (gyms, changing rooms, some bars) restrict or ban them. If you're buying a pair, factor in that:

  • People around you didn't consent to being filmed — be upfront when you're recording, and turn them off in places where it matters.
  • Voice AI queries are usually sent to the cloud for processing, meaning what you ask (and what the camera sees) may be logged by the manufacturer, depending on their privacy settings.
  • Losing them is a bigger deal than losing sunglasses — check what's recoverable remotely if they support device-finding.

Who should buy, who should skip

They suit people who already wear glasses or sunglasses daily and want hands-free photo/video and quick AI lookups without pulling out a phone — cyclists, parents at kids' sports, travellers wanting quick translation. They're a harder sell if you don't normally wear eyewear, since prescription lens fitting and case-carrying add friction, or if all-day screen-on features matter more to you than a phone can already do.

In New Zealand, Meta's Ray-Ban glasses are increasingly stocked through JB Hi-Fi and select opticians, with PB Tech and Noel Leeming carrying related audio-glasses hybrids from other brands. Compare current stock and pricing before committing, since availability of specific frame styles and prescription options varies store to store.

Before you buy: check return policy specifically for prescription-fitted units — swapping lenses after purchase is often not straightforward, and restocking fees can apply.
