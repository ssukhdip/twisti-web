Best Coffee Grinder NZ: Which One to Buy

A good grinder does more for your coffee than almost any other piece of equipment, including the machine itself. Here's how to choose one that actually fits your brew method and budget.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

Burr vs blade: not actually a close call

Blade grinders chop beans unevenly with spinning blades, producing a mix of coarse chunks and fine dust in the same batch. This uneven grind leads to inconsistent extraction — some particles over-extract and taste bitter while others under-extract and taste sour — no matter how good your beans or machine are. Burr grinders crush beans between two abrasive surfaces to a consistent size, which is genuinely one of the biggest single upgrades you can make to home coffee quality.

If you're serious about coffee at all, a burr grinder is worth prioritising over almost any other equipment upgrade, including a fancier espresso machine.

Flat burrs vs conical burrs

Flat burr grinders tend to produce a very consistent, uniform particle size and are common in higher-end espresso-focused grinders, though they're often noisier and pricier. Conical burr grinders are generally quieter and more affordable, and produce excellent results for filter and plunger brewing, with slightly more variation in particle size that matters less for these brew methods. For most home users doing filter or plunger coffee, conical burr grinders offer very good value.

Electric vs hand grinders

Electric burr grinders are faster and more convenient for daily use, especially if you're grinding for a household or before work. Hand grinders have improved dramatically in recent years, with some higher-end manual models now producing espresso-quality consistency that rivals mid-range electric grinders, at a lower price point since you're not paying for a motor. The trade-off is time and effort — hand grinding a full pot's worth of coffee takes a couple of minutes of physical effort, every time.

  • Filter/plunger brewing: a mid-range conical burr grinder (electric or hand) is usually all you need.
  • Espresso: invest in a grinder with fine, precise grind adjustment — this matters more for espresso than any other brew method.
  • Grinding for one or two people: a good hand grinder can be genuinely competitive with electric at a lower price.

Grind adjustment and consistency

Look for a grinder with a wide range of adjustment steps, particularly if you plan to brew multiple ways (say, espresso and plunger). Stepped adjustment (click settings) is more common and more predictable to dial in repeatably than infinitely variable/stepless adjustment, though stepless gives you more precise fine-tuning once you learn it — this matters most for espresso, where tiny grind changes noticeably affect extraction.

Maintenance tip: Burrs wear out and dull over years of use regardless of brand, and dirty grinders build up stale coffee oils that affect taste. Check whether replacement burrs are available for your chosen grinder before buying, particularly for pricier electric models you intend to keep long-term.

Price expectations in NZ

Basic entry-level burr grinders (hand or electric) sit at the affordable end, mid-range models with better adjustment and burr quality cost meaningfully more, and premium espresso-focused grinders with precise stepless adjustment sit at the top of the market. Spending more on the grinder rather than the machine is often the better trade-off for improving your actual cup quality.
