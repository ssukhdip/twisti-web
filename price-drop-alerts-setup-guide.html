Setting up price drop alerts: a step-by-step guide

Waiting for a price to drop shouldn't mean refreshing a product page every day. Here's how to set up alerts properly so the price finds you instead.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

Why manual checking doesn't work

Retail pricing in New Zealand moves more than most shoppers realise. A laptop or TV can shift price several times in a month as retailers respond to stock levels, competitor moves, and short sales windows. If you're relying on memory or the odd manual check, you'll miss most of the good windows entirely — sales here often run for a few days, not weeks.

Price drop alerts solve this by doing the watching for you. Instead of you checking the page, the system checks it on a schedule and only pings you when something changes in your favour.

Setting up an alert on Twisti

The simplest approach for NZ shoppers is to track the product across multiple retailers at once rather than watching a single store. That way you're not just waiting for a price cut — you're also catching the moment another retailer becomes cheaper.

  • Find the product on Twisti and open its comparison page, which lists current pricing across PB Tech, Noel Leeming, JB Hi-Fi, Harvey Norman and others.
  • Set a target price or simply opt in to be notified of any price change.
  • Choose how you want to be notified — email is the most reliable option for most people.
  • Leave it running. You don't need to check back until you get a notification.

Single-retailer watch vs multi-retailer watch

Watching one retailer's product page only tells you when that store changes its price. Watching across retailers tells you the moment the item becomes cheapest anywhere — which matters more, since the cheapest seller for a given product often changes hands over a sale period.

What counts as a genuine drop

Not every "sale" price is actually lower than what the item has sold for recently. Some retailers run promotions off an inflated "was" price that the item rarely, if ever, sold at. This is exactly why price history matters alongside alerts — an alert tells you the price moved, but a history chart tells you whether that movement is meaningful.

Before buying on an alert, it's worth a quick check of how the price has trended over the past month or two. A "30% off" sticker means very little if the item was quietly discounted to the same level a few weeks earlier.

Tuning your alerts so they're useful, not noisy

If you set alerts too loosely — for example, on a broad category rather than a specific product — you'll end up with an inbox full of notifications you start ignoring. A few practical tips:

  • Track specific models, not general categories, when you know what you want.
  • Set a realistic target price based on the item's recent price range, not its RRP.
  • If you're not in a hurry, widen your patience — big-ticket electronics (TVs, laptops, appliances) tend to see their best pricing around sale periods like Boxing Day, Black Friday and end-of-financial-year clearances.
Caveat: Alerts tell you when a price changes, not whether it's the lowest it will ever be. Stock levels, model run-outs and one-off promotions all affect timing, so treat an alert as a prompt to check the full picture rather than an instant buy signal.

When to act on an alert

If the price has hit your target and you've confirmed it against recent history, there's rarely a strong reason to keep waiting — especially on popular items that sell out fast during sales. Where it pays to be patient is on items you have flexibility around, like appliances outside of winter or laptops outside of the back-to-school rush.
