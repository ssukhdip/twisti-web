Power Bank Airline Rules for NZ Flights

Pack the wrong power bank and it can be confiscated at security or refused at the gate. Here's exactly what's allowed on NZ flights and where.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026
How we picked these: Rules below are based on Air New Zealand's published dangerous goods policy and the general CAA/IATA lithium battery guidelines that most airlines operating out of NZ follow.

The core rule: carry-on only

Power banks are lithium-ion batteries, and airlines worldwide — including Air New Zealand — do not allow them in checked baggage at all. They must be carried in your cabin bag. This isn't a NZ-specific quirk; it's a global aviation safety rule because a battery fire in the cargo hold is much harder for crew to detect and fight than one in the cabin.

Watt-hour (Wh) limits

Airline rules are based on watt-hours (Wh), not the mAh rating printed on most consumer power banks — you may need to do a quick conversion (Wh = mAh × voltage ÷ 1000, using the battery's actual voltage, commonly 3.7V for a single cell). The general bands used by Air New Zealand and most international carriers are:

  • Under 100Wh (roughly under 27,000mAh at 3.7V) — allowed in carry-on without special approval, no quantity limit beyond general reasonableness.
  • 100–160Wh — allowed in carry-on, but you typically need airline approval in advance, and most airlines cap you at two such devices.
  • Over 160Wh — not permitted on passenger aircraft at all, carry-on or checked.

Most power banks sold in NZ through JB Hi-Fi, Noel Leeming, PB Tech, and The Warehouse — including popular 20,000mAh and 26,800mAh models from Anker, Belkin, and Xiaomi — sit comfortably under the 100Wh threshold, so this rarely becomes an issue for typical travel power banks.

Why some get confiscated anyway

The most common reason a power bank gets pulled at security isn't the Wh rating — it's a missing or illegible Wh/mAh label. Security staff can't verify a battery is within limits if the rating isn't printed clearly on the device, and unlabelled or heavily worn power banks are often confiscated purely on that basis, regardless of actual capacity. Cheap, generic power banks bought without clear NZ retail packaging are the most common casualties here.

Damaged power banks — swollen casing, exposed wiring, visible battery bulge — will also be refused, as will any device an airline judges to be a counterfeit or non-compliant battery pack.

Practical tips for NZ travellers

  • Check the Wh rating is printed on the device before you fly — if it only shows mAh, do the conversion yourself and keep a note of it in case you're asked.
  • Buy from a retailer with clear specs rather than an unbranded marketplace listing, so the Wh figure is documented if questioned.
  • Keep it accessible in carry-on, not buried in checked luggage, and be ready to show it separately at security screening if asked.
  • For international connections, note that some countries (and airlines beyond Air NZ) apply the same 100Wh/160Wh bands but enforce them more strictly — worth checking the specific carrier's page if you're on a multi-leg itinerary.

Bottom line

A standard, clearly-labelled 20,000mAh power bank from a mainstream NZ retailer will almost always clear security without issue — the Wh limits mostly matter for people carrying oversized battery packs (like camera or laptop power banks above 27,000mAh) or unbranded gear without proper labelling.
