Are Extended Warranties Worth It in NZ?

The checkout upsell every electronics retailer asks about. Here's what it actually buys you on top of your existing legal rights, and when it's genuinely worth the money.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

You already have more cover than the salesperson mentions

Under the Consumer Guarantees Act (CGA), every product sold in New Zealand has to be of "acceptable quality" and last a reasonable time for the type of product and price paid. That protection exists automatically, for free, regardless of whether you buy an extended warranty - and it isn't capped at 12 months. A $2,000 laptop failing after 18 months, for example, could reasonably be expected to last longer than that under the CGA, independent of any manufacturer warranty length.

The catch is that CGA claims go through the retailer, can involve some back-and-forth about what's "reasonable," and don't cover accidental damage, misuse, or normal wear and tear. That gap is exactly what extended warranties are sold to fill.

What an extended warranty typically adds

  • Longer time window: cover that runs past the manufacturer's standard 12–24 months, often out to 3–5 years.
  • Accidental damage: drops, spills, and cracked screens - the most common real-world failure mode, and something the CGA generally doesn't touch.
  • No-argument replacement: a defined process instead of a CGA negotiation about what counts as a reasonable lifespan.
  • Loan or express replacement: some plans include a loan unit or faster turnaround while yours is being repaired.

CGA vs extended warranty vs manufacturer warranty

Manufacturer warranties (usually 12–24 months) cover manufacturing defects only, and are free with every purchase. The CGA sits on top of that, is also free, and covers a broader "acceptable quality" standard for a period the Act treats as reasonable for the item - which can be well beyond the manufacturer's stated warranty for anything actually built to last. An extended warranty is the only one of the three you pay for, and its main value-add is accidental damage cover plus a fixed, argument-free process rather than a CGA claim.

When it's genuinely worth paying for

Extended warranties earn their keep in a fairly narrow set of situations. High-use, drop-prone devices - phones, laptops that travel daily, gaming handhelds - carry real accidental-damage risk that no free legal right covers. Expensive appliances with moving parts and motors, like robot vacuums or espresso machines, can be costly to repair out of warranty, and a family with kids or a chaotic kitchen genuinely raises the odds of an accidental claim.

It also matters who's selling the reliability story. If a category has a known failure pattern - certain robot vacuum brushes, certain laptop hinges - and the extended warranty is priced modestly relative to the replacement cost, the maths can work out. The reverse is also true: an extremely reliable, low-drop-risk item (a wall-mounted TV, a desktop PC that never moves) is a weaker candidate.

Do the maths before saying yes at the counter: a rough rule of thumb is that an extended warranty is worth considering when its cost is under roughly 10–15% of the item's price and it covers a failure mode - usually accidental damage - that the CGA doesn't. Above that, you're often better off self-insuring: put the same amount aside and it covers a genuine repair or replacement if and when you need one, with the balance left over if you never do.

Questions worth asking before you buy one

  • Does it cover accidental damage, or only mechanical/electrical failure the CGA might already cover?
  • Is there an excess or fee per claim, and does that change the real value?
  • How many claims are allowed, and is there a claim cap in dollar terms?
  • Does the plan follow the product if you sell it, or is it non-transferable?
  • Who administers the claim - the retailer directly, or a third-party insurer with its own approval process?

A cheaper alternative worth knowing about

Some credit cards in NZ automatically extend a manufacturer's warranty by an extra 12 months when you pay with that card, at no additional cost. It's worth checking your own card's terms before paying for an extended warranty that might partially duplicate cover you already have.

Compare the sticker price first

Whatever you decide on the warranty, make sure the underlying price is actually competitive across NZ retailers before you check out.
