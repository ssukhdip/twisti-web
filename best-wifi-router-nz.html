Best Wi-Fi Router NZ: Which One Should You Actually Buy

Five real routers, real NZ prices, and the honest case for why the cheap one is right for most people.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

What actually matters when you're picking a router

Most router marketing is noise. Strip it back and there are really only four things that decide whether you'll be happy: the Wi-Fi standard (6, 6E or 7), how much floor area and how many devices it's realistically rated for, the port selection on the back, and whether you actually need the speed on offer. A gigabit fibre plan maxes out well before most routers do, so buying for raw Wi-Fi 7 headline numbers alone is often money spent on a spec you'll never touch.

If your house is a standard three-bedroom and your internet plan is anything up to a gigabit, a solid Wi-Fi 6 router will not be the bottleneck. If you've got 30+ connected devices, multiple people gaming or streaming 4K at once, or you've future-proofed with a multi-gig fibre plan, that's when Wi-Fi 7 and better hardware start to earn their keep.

The picks

TP-Link Archer AX55 (AX3000) -- around $99 to $129

This is the router we point most people to first. It's Wi-Fi 6, dual-band, handles a typical household of 15 to 20 devices without breaking a sweat, and TP-Link's Tether app is genuinely easy to use for parental controls and guest networks. It won't win any speed contests but for a two to three bedroom place with a normal fibre plan, it's very hard to justify spending more. Suits: renters, first apartment, anyone replacing an ISP-supplied modem-router combo that's been struggling.

TP-Link Archer AX1800 -- around $75 to $89

The cheapest sensible option if budget is the priority. Fewer antennas and a smaller effective range than the AX55, so it's really a one or two person apartment router rather than a family home router. Four gigabit LAN ports is still a nice inclusion at this price. Suits: small flats, a spare router for the bach, or a backup if your main router ever dies.

ASUS RT-AX86U (or current equivalent) -- around $329 to $379

The step up for households with a lot going on -- gaming consoles, multiple work-from-home laptops on video calls, smart TVs and a NAS all fighting for bandwidth at once. ASUS's QoS and gaming-prioritisation tools are the best in the business at this price point, and the 2.5G WAN port matters if you're on a faster-than-gigabit fibre plan. Suits: families with 4+ heavy users, anyone who games competitively and hates lag spikes when someone else starts a Netflix binge.

Netgear Nighthawk RS300 (Wi-Fi 7) -- around $500 to $550

A genuinely useful entry into Wi-Fi 7 rather than a gimmick -- the wider channels and lower latency show up if you've got a multi-gig fibre plan and modern Wi-Fi 7 devices (recent iPhones, Samsung Galaxy S25/S26, newer laptops) to take advantage of it. If your gear is mostly two or three years old, you won't notice much difference over a good Wi-Fi 6 router yet. Suits: early adopters and anyone already paying for 2Gbps+ fibre who wants the router to stop being the limiting factor.

ASUS RT-BE88U (Wi-Fi 7) -- around $600 to $650

The one to buy if you want headroom for the next five years and aren't fussed about price. Multi-gig ports front and back, serious throughput for large households, and enough processing power that it won't feel dated when more of your devices catch up to Wi-Fi 7. Overkill for most people in 2026 -- but if you've already been burned by a router that couldn't keep up, this is the one you buy once.

Common mistake: buying a router rated for a much bigger house than you have. Router range specs are measured in ideal open-plan conditions -- real NZ homes with weatherboard walls, hot water cylinders and the odd brick chimney will always underperform the box's claims. If you're in a two-storey house or anything with thick internal walls, a mesh system (see our mesh vs range extender guide) will usually solve coverage problems better than a single more expensive router.

Ports and features worth checking before you buy

  • WAN port speed -- if your fibre plan is faster than 1Gbps, make sure the router has a 2.5G or faster WAN port, or you'll bottleneck your own connection.
  • Number of LAN ports -- four gigabit ports is standard; if you've got a games console, smart TV and desktop PC all wired in, don't go for a router with only two.
  • USB port -- handy for sharing a printer or an external drive across the network, missing on the cheapest models.
  • Firmware update history -- TP-Link, ASUS and Netgear all have decent track records; avoid no-name brands that stop pushing security updates within a year.

The bottom line

For most Kiwi households, the TP-Link Archer AX55 or something in that exact class is the sensible buy -- it's the one that answers "will this be a problem in six months" with a confident no, without spending money on speed you can't use. Go up the range only once you can name the specific reason you need more (a genuinely large house, a house full of gamers, or a fibre plan faster than gigabit).
