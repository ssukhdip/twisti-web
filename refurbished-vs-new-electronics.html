Refurbished vs New Electronics: Is It Worth It?

Refurbished can be a genuinely smart buy or a false economy, depending entirely on the category and the seller. Here's how to tell which one you're looking at before you hand over your card.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

"Refurbished" isn't one thing

The word gets stretched to cover a lot of very different products, and the gap in quality between them is the whole ballgame. A manufacturer-refurbished unit (Apple, Samsung, Dell and similar selling direct) has typically been inspected, had worn parts replaced, and comes with a proper warranty - this is the safest tier. A retailer-certified refurb from a major NZ electronics retailer sits a notch below, usually with a shorter warranty and a published grading scale. Then there's third-party or marketplace "refurbished", which can mean anything from a genuinely reconditioned unit to a lightly wiped second-hand device with a new sticker - grading and warranty here vary wildly seller to seller, so the label alone tells you very little.

Where the savings are usually real

Refurbished pricing tends to reflect genuine cost avoidance for the seller - no new packaging, no full retail margin, and stock that would otherwise sit unsold - so a meaningful discount off the equivalent new price is normal and legitimate, not a red flag by itself. The size of that discount varies a lot by category and by how recently the model launched, so it's worth checking the current new price for the equivalent model before assuming any refurbished listing is automatically a bargain.

Categories where refurbished makes the most sense

  • Laptops, especially MacBooks: long support windows, minimal moving parts to fail, and a well-established resale and refurb market with clear grading standards.
  • Phones one or two generations back: once a phone is no longer the newest model, refurbished units from reputable sellers are usually well tested and the software support runway is still long.
  • Monitors and desktop PCs: display panels and desktop components degrade slowly and predictably, making condition easy to verify against a spec sheet.

Categories where it's a harder sell

  • The newest flagship phone or laptop: if it just launched, "refurbished" stock is almost always a return or a display unit rather than a genuine cost-driven discount - the saving versus new is usually too small to justify losing full warranty and packaging.
  • Appliances with moving parts or heating elements: vacuums, air fryers, coffee machines - mechanical wear is harder to assess from a listing photo, and failure is more disruptive than with a laptop.
  • Wearables and anything with a sealed, non-replaceable battery: battery health on a used smartwatch or set of earbuds is very hard to verify before purchase, and a rapidly degrading battery is often the actual reason it was returned.

The one number that matters most: battery health

For phones, laptops and wearables, battery condition is the single biggest hidden variable in any refurbished purchase. A reputable refurbisher will disclose battery health as a percentage or cycle count up front. If a listing has no battery information at all, treat that as a gap to ask about directly rather than an oversight to ignore - it's usually the first thing a legitimate refurbisher will volunteer.

Five things to check before you buy refurbished

  • Warranty length and who provides it - a manufacturer or retailer warranty is worth more than a marketplace seller's personal guarantee.
  • The grading scale used - "Grade A / like new," "Grade B / good," and so on mean different things to different sellers, so read the seller's own definition rather than assuming a universal standard.
  • Return window - a proper return period (rather than "all sales final") is one of the clearest signs of a seller confident in their own grading.
  • Battery health disclosure, for anything with a battery.
  • Included accessories - genuine charger, cable, and box, versus a bare unit - since aftermarket accessories are where a lot of quality complaints actually originate.
Common misconception: a lot of shoppers assume "refurbished" automatically means "broken and fixed." In most legitimate cases it means the unit was returned unused within a short window, inspected, and resold - functionally new, just without the shrink-wrap and full retail price.

How to actually decide

Before treating any refurbished listing as a deal, check what the equivalent new unit costs right now across the major NZ retailers - if the refurbished discount is small once you account for a shorter warranty and no packaging, new is usually the better call. If the gap is substantial and the seller discloses grading, warranty and battery health clearly, refurbished is very often the smarter buy.
