Best Car Jump Starter NZ: Which to Buy

A flat battery on a wet Wellington morning is a bad time to discover your jumper cables are in the shed and there's nobody around to help. Here's what actually matters when picking a jump starter.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

Lithium jump starters vs old-school jumper cables

Traditional jumper cables are cheap and they work, but they need a second running car and someone who knows which terminal is which. A lithium jump starter is a battery pack the size of a large power bank that clamps straight onto your dead battery — no second vehicle required. For most people who drive daily and occasionally get caught out by lights left on or a battery on its last legs, a lithium unit is worth the money. If you mostly drive with a partner or in a household with two cars, cables might genuinely be enough.

The trade-off is that lithium jump starters self-discharge over months sitting in the boot, so they need an occasional top-up charge — check yours every couple of months if it's not seen use.

Peak amps: what you actually need

Every jump starter is sold on its peak amp rating, and it's the number most people get wrong. Peak amps is the maximum burst current, not a sustained figure, and manufacturers inflate these numbers more than almost any other spec in consumer electronics.

  • Small petrol four-cylinder (e.g. Yaris, Swift): 400-600A peak is genuinely enough.
  • Mid-size sedan or SUV (Corolla, RAV4, Mazda 6): aim for 800-1000A peak.
  • Six-cylinder, V8, or diesel ute/4WD (Hilux, Ranger, Prado): 1500-2000A peak, and check it explicitly lists diesel support — diesel engines need more cranking current.
  • Big diesel trucks or anything with a large-capacity battery: go for the highest-rated unit you can find, or stick with cables and another vehicle.

If in doubt, buy one rating bracket above what you think you need. Cold mornings and an ageing battery both eat into your safety margin.

Where to buy one in NZ

PB Tech and Torpedo7 carry a wide range of budget-to-mid units, Repco and Supercheap Auto stock dedicated automotive brands like NOCO and Ring which are well regarded for their safety certification, and Noel Leeming and Harvey Norman stock general consumer electronics brands that double as power banks. Torpedo7 and Repco tend to have the most jump-starter-specific range; the big electronics retailers are better if you also want the unit to double as a phone power bank.

Safety features actually worth paying for

Cheap jump starters and cheap jumper cables both carry real risk — reverse polarity, sparking near a battery that's off-gassing hydrogen, and dead-short damage to your car's electronics are all genuine failure modes, not scaremongering.

  • Reverse polarity protection: non-negotiable. It won't let current flow if you clamp the wrong terminal.
  • Spark-proof clamps: reduce ignition risk when connecting near battery gases.
  • Short-circuit and overcurrent protection: protects your car's ECU and the jump starter itself.
  • IP-rated casing: if it's living in a ute tray or boot exposed to weather, look for at least IP54.
Honest take: a no-name jump starter with none of the above safety features is a genuine risk to your car's electronics on modern vehicles packed with sensitive modules. This is one category where paying an extra $30-50 for a known automotive brand with proper certification is worth it over the cheapest option on a marketplace listing.

Other things worth checking

Look for a built-in torch (genuinely useful at night), a USB output to charge your phone while you wait for roadside assistance, and a battery percentage display so you're not guessing whether it has enough charge left. Cable length matters too — cheap units often ship with clamps on very short leads that make it awkward to reach some engine bays.
