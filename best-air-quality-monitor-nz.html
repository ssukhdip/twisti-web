Best Air Quality Monitor NZ: Which to Buy

Awair, IQAir and Xiaomi all promise to tell you what's in your home's air. Here's which pollutants actually matter for NZ houses, and how a monitor should connect to a purifier or HRV system rather than just sit on a shelf.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

Which pollutants actually matter in an NZ home

Not every sensor a monitor advertises is equally relevant here. Three stand out for typical New Zealand housing conditions. First, humidity and the mould/damp risk that comes with it — a huge number of older NZ homes, especially villas and 1960s-90s builds without good insulation, run persistently damp in winter, and humidity readings are a genuinely useful early warning before mould becomes visible. Second, PM2.5 (fine particulate matter), which spikes noticeably in winter in wood-fire-heavy areas — Christchurch, Nelson, and plenty of smaller towns still see real smoke events on still winter evenings, and a monitor placed near a living room with an open fire will show it clearly. Third, CO2, which climbs in bedrooms and living rooms with poor ventilation, particularly in newer, tightly-sealed homes without adequate ventilation systems — elevated CO2 doesn't just mean "stuffy," it's linked to reduced concentration and poorer sleep quality. VOCs (from cleaning products, new furniture, paint) are worth having as a secondary metric but matter less day-to-day for most households than the three above.

What each pollutant reading actually tells you

  • Humidity above roughly 60-65%: Sustained readings in this range are a strong signal of mould risk, particularly in bathrooms, bedrooms with poor airflow, and any room with an exterior wall that's poorly insulated.
  • PM2.5 spikes: Track alongside wood burner use, nearby neighbours' fires, or busy roads — a spike that appears every evening in winter is very likely combustion smoke, indoor or out.
  • CO2 above roughly 1000-1500ppm: A common sign a room needs a window cracked or better ventilation, especially bedrooms overnight with the door closed.
  • VOCs: Useful for catching one-off events (new paint, cleaning sprays, new furniture off-gassing) rather than an ongoing daily concern.
  • Temperature: Not a pollutant, but most monitors track it anyway, and it's genuinely useful cross-referenced against humidity for damp risk.

How monitors pair with purifiers and HRV systems

A standalone monitor that just displays numbers on an app is useful for diagnosis, but the real value comes from pairing it with something that can actually act on the data. Air purifiers from brands that support smart integration can ramp fan speed automatically when PM2.5 climbs, rather than running at a fixed speed regardless of conditions — genuinely useful during a smoky winter evening when you don't want to babysit a purifier's settings manually. Whole-home HRV (heat/energy recovery ventilation) systems, increasingly common in newer NZ builds and popular retrofits, benefit from a CO2 and humidity monitor to confirm the system is actually doing its job of exchanging stale, humid air for fresh air, rather than just trusting the installer's promise that it's "handling it." If you're deciding between multiple monitors, check that it uses a communication protocol (Wi-Fi, Matter, or the purifier brand's own ecosystem) compatible with any purifier or HRV control system you already have or plan to buy.

Worth noting: cheaper monitors sometimes use lower-grade PM2.5 sensors that read reasonably well relatively (showing when things get worse) but aren't as accurate in absolute terms — fine for spotting trends and problem rooms, less reliable if you want to compare your reading against official outdoor air quality index numbers.

Where a monitor is genuinely worth it vs unnecessary

Worth it: older, harder-to-heat homes with a history of damp or mould, households with a wood burner or open fire, anyone with respiratory conditions like asthma where knowing about smoke or humidity spikes has real practical value, and newer airtight homes where nobody's actually checked whether the ventilation system is keeping CO2 in check. Less essential: a modern, well-ventilated home with no damp history and no combustion heating — you'll likely already sense stuffiness or dampness before a monitor tells you anything you don't already suspect. Either way, a monitor doesn't fix anything by itself; its value is entirely in prompting you (or your purifier/HRV system) to act on what it shows.
