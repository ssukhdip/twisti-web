Best Gaming Headset NZ: Which One to Buy

A gaming headset is really three separate decisions: connection type, driver design, and microphone quality. Here's what actually affects your gameplay and what's just marketing noise.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

Wired vs wireless — does latency actually matter?

Modern wireless gaming headsets using dedicated 2.4GHz USB dongles have genuinely low latency, low enough that it's not a meaningful disadvantage for the vast majority of players, including competitive ones. Bluetooth is a different story — Bluetooth audio latency is noticeably higher and can throw off timing-sensitive games, so avoid relying on Bluetooth mode for anything competitive. If you're buying wireless, check it uses a proper low-latency 2.4GHz dongle rather than Bluetooth-only, and budget for charging habits since battery life varies a lot between models.

Open-back vs closed-back

Closed-back headsets seal around your ear, blocking outside noise and stopping sound leaking out — better for shared spaces, flatting situations, or late-night gaming without disturbing others. Open-back headsets let air (and sound) pass through the ear cups, which creates a more natural, spacious soundstage that's genuinely better for pinpointing directional audio in competitive shooters, but leaks sound loudly and lets outside noise in. If you live with others or game at night, closed-back is the practical choice regardless of the soundstage trade-off.

Soundstage and positional audio

For competitive games where hearing footsteps and gunfire direction matters, headsets with genuinely good driver quality and virtual surround (or open-back design) give you a real advantage. Cheap headsets often muddy directional cues even with "7.1 surround" marketed on the box — real positional accuracy comes from driver and tuning quality, not a surround sound logo. If competitive audio cues matter to you, read dedicated audio reviews rather than trusting the feature list alone.

Mic quality — what your teammates actually notice

  • Clarity over "quality": your teammates care whether they can understand you clearly over game audio, not whether the mic sounds studio-grade.
  • Background noise handling: a mic with decent noise cancelling or a directional cardioid pattern cuts down on keyboard clatter and fan noise reaching your team.
  • Detachable/flip-to-mute mics: genuinely useful — lets you quickly mute without fumbling for a software button mid-game.
  • Boom mic position: a mic that sits too far from your mouth (common on cheaper headsets) sounds thin and distant even if the mic itself is decent quality.

Comfort for long sessions

If you game for hours at a time, clamping force and ear cup padding matter more day-to-day than almost any audio spec. Headsets that feel fine in a 5-minute shop demo can become genuinely uncomfortable after two hours — check reviews specifically mentioning long-session comfort, and be wary of very lightweight "gaming" headsets with thin padding that looks good but doesn't hold up.

Honest take: don't overspend on wireless latency claims if you're not playing competitively at a serious level — a good wired headset is cheaper, has zero charging hassle, and sounds identical for the vast majority of players. Save the wireless premium for when cable management genuinely bothers you.
