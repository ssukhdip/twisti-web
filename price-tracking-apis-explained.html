Price Tracking APIs Explained (For the Curious)

Ever wondered how a site like Twisti actually knows current prices across multiple retailers? Here's a plain-English look at how price tracking APIs work.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

What a price tracking API actually does

At a basic level, an API (application programming interface) is a structured way for one piece of software to request data from another. In the context of price comparison, this typically means regularly requesting current price and stock information for specific products from retailer websites or their own data feeds, then storing that information so it can be displayed and compared in one place.

How comparison sites gather pricing data

There are a few common approaches: some retailers provide structured data feeds or affiliate APIs specifically designed for this purpose, while other data is gathered by periodically checking public product pages. Reputable comparison tools generally aim to respect each retailer's terms of use and rate limits when collecting this information, and refresh data on a regular schedule rather than constantly, to keep results useful without overloading retailer systems.

Why data freshness matters

A comparison tool is only as useful as its last price check. If prices are pulled once a day versus once an hour, that gap can matter during a fast-moving sale event like Black Friday, so it's worth understanding roughly how current the data you're looking at actually is.

If you're curious about building your own

For anyone with basic coding experience who wants to experiment, building a small personal price tracker is a genuinely approachable project — checking a handful of product pages on a schedule and logging the results isn't technically difficult. That said, it's worth being mindful of each site's terms of service, since large-scale or aggressive automated requests can be against a retailer's terms or even blocked outright.

  • Check whether the retailer offers an official API or affiliate feed before resorting to scraping their pages directly.
  • Keep request frequency reasonable — checking a price every few hours is usually more than enough for personal use.
  • Be aware that page structures change over time, so any manual tracking setup will likely need occasional maintenance.

Why this matters for everyday shoppers

You don't need to understand any of this to use a comparison site effectively, but knowing roughly how the data gets there helps explain why prices sometimes lag by a few hours, and why a dedicated comparison tool with broad retailer coverage tends to be more reliable than trying to track everything by hand.

Good to know: Prices shown on comparison sites are a helpful guide, but it's always worth confirming the final price directly with the retailer at checkout, since stock levels and pricing can change between when a page was last checked and when you complete a purchase.

Bottom line

Price tracking APIs are simply the plumbing behind comparison sites — a structured, repeated way of gathering pricing data so it can be compared quickly. Understanding the basics helps set realistic expectations about how current any comparison tool's data really is.
