Build Your Own Price Tracker with a Spreadsheet

If you'd rather not rely on a third-party tool, a simple spreadsheet can do a surprisingly good job of tracking electronics prices over time. Here's a practical setup.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

Why bother with a manual tracker

Automated comparison tools are convenient, but a personal spreadsheet gives you full control over exactly which items and retailers you're watching, and it costs nothing beyond a bit of your time. It's particularly useful for a specific, planned purchase — like a TV or laptop you're saving up for — where you want to understand normal pricing before committing.

Setting up the basic structure

A simple layout works best: one row per price check, with columns for date, retailer, product name, model number, price, and a notes column for anything relevant (like "included free shipping" or "bundle deal"). Keeping model numbers precise matters more than it might seem, since near-identical products can have meaningfully different specs.

Minimum columns to include

Date checked, retailer name, exact model number, price in NZD, and whether shipping was included. These five fields are usually enough to spot genuine trends and avoid comparing apples to oranges later.

How often to check and log

Checking weekly is usually enough to catch meaningful price movement without becoming a chore — daily tracking rarely reveals much more unless you're watching for a specific known sale event. Around major sale periods (Black Friday, Boxing Day, winter sales) it's worth checking more frequently, since prices can shift quickly during these windows.

  • Set a recurring calendar reminder rather than relying on memory to check prices.
  • Log the price even when it hasn't changed — this confirms your data isn't stale.
  • Note any conditions attached to a price (bundle, trade-in required, membership pricing).

Turning data into a decision

Once you've got a few weeks of data, a simple chart of price against date can make it obvious whether current pricing is actually good relative to what you've observed, or just an average price with sale-style marketing around it. This is the main value of doing this yourself — you're building a personal reference point rather than trusting a retailer's own "was/now" framing.

Time-saving tip: If tracking multiple items becomes tedious, it's usually more efficient to switch to a dedicated comparison tool for broad browsing, and reserve the spreadsheet approach for one or two specific big-ticket purchases you're watching closely.

Bottom line

A basic spreadsheet is a genuinely effective, zero-cost way to understand whether a price is actually good, especially for a single planned purchase. It takes a bit of discipline to maintain, but the data it produces is entirely your own and fully trustworthy.
