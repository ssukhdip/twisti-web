Best Tyre Inflator NZ: Which One to Buy

Petrol station air pumps are getting harder to find free and working properly. A decent portable inflator in the boot pays for itself the first time a warning light comes on before a long drive.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

12V plug-in vs cordless inflators

A 12V inflator plugs into your car's accessory socket and draws power from the engine running, which means unlimited runtime but a cord that limits how far you can reach — awkward on a trailer or a second car parked away from a socket. Cordless (battery) inflators are more flexible and can air up bikes, sports balls, and paddleboards away from a vehicle entirely, but battery capacity limits how many tyres you can do in one charge, and they're generally slower per tyre than a 12V unit.

For most households, a 12V inflator that lives in the glovebox is the practical choice. If you've got e-bikes, kids' bikes, an inflatable kayak, or a boat, a cordless unit earns its keep well beyond the car.

Is the built-in gauge actually accurate?

This is the single biggest quality difference between a $30 inflator and a $100 one. Cheap analogue-style digital gauges on budget inflators can be out by 3-5 PSI, which matters — a genuine underinflation of that amount affects handling, fuel use, and tyre wear over time, and an overinflation reading that's actually correct might make you let air out when you shouldn't.

  • Auto shut-off inflators: set your target PSI and they stop automatically — convenient, but only as good as the gauge doing the measuring.
  • A cheap separate tyre gauge (under $15) is worth having regardless — use it to spot-check any inflator's reading, especially on a new unit, before trusting it fully.
  • Digital displays with 0.1 PSI increments are nice to have but not proof of accuracy — resolution and accuracy are different things.
Honest take: don't assume a higher price automatically means a more accurate gauge. Cross-check any new inflator against a dedicated pen or dial gauge on your first use. If it's out by more than 1-2 PSI, that's worth returning under warranty rather than living with.

Emergency kit vs everyday convenience

If you're buying purely as a breakdown-prevention tool — something that lives in the boot for the rare flat or slow leak — a basic auto-shut-off 12V unit under $50 does the job fine. If you plan to actually maintain correct tyre pressure month to month (which most people should do and don't), spend a bit more on one with a genuinely accurate gauge and a stable base or hose that doesn't fight you while you're crouched at the wheel.

Where to buy one in NZ

Repco and Supercheap Auto specialise in automotive inflators and stock the widest range of dedicated 12V units, often from Ring or their own house brands. PB Tech and Torpedo7 carry cordless multi-purpose inflators that double for sports equipment and bikes. Kmart and Briscoes sell basic budget units that are fine for occasional emergency use but rarely have the gauge accuracy for regular pressure maintenance.

A few extra things to check

  • Cord/hose length: shorter than 3m plug-in cords struggle to reach a rear tyre on larger vehicles.
  • Noise level: cheaper compressors are noticeably louder — check reviews if you'll be using it near neighbours or at night.
  • Included nozzles: useful if you also want to inflate sports balls, mattresses, or pool toys.
