BUYING GUIDE

Best Robot Vacuum NZ

LiDAR, self-empty bases, mopping rollers - the spec sheet reads like sci-fi. Here's what actually matters and which models are worth your money in 2026.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

What actually matters when you're buying a robot vacuum

Robot vacuums have quietly gone from novelty to genuinely useful over the last few years, but the spec sheets are a mess of marketing numbers. Here's what's worth paying attention to and what's mostly noise.

  • Suction power (Pa): anything from 3,000Pa up handles hard floors and light carpet fine. If you've got thick carpet, a shedding dog, or both, look for 5,000Pa-plus or a model with carpet-boost detection that automatically ramps up.
  • Mapping and navigation: LiDAR (a spinning laser turret) maps rooms accurately and avoids furniture legs and cables better than camera-only or gyroscope-based systems. Budget models without LiDAR still work, they just bump around more and take longer.
  • Self-empty base: this is the single biggest quality-of-life upgrade in the category. A base that sucks the bin empty into a bag means you're not touching dust for weeks at a time instead of every second day.
  • Mopping: genuinely useful for daily maintenance on hard floors, not a substitute for a proper mop. Look for models that lift the mop pad automatically when they detect carpet, otherwise you'll get soggy rugs.
  • Battery runtime: 150-180 minutes on a single charge is standard and enough for most NZ homes. Bigger houses want auto-resume, where the robot finishes charging and picks up where it left off rather than calling the job done early.

Ecovacs Deebot T-series (T80 / T90 Omni range)

Ecovacs has become the value leader in the mid-to-premium bracket, and the T-series Omni models are usually sitting somewhere in the $1,100-$1,600 range at Noel Leeming and JB Hi-Fi depending on which generation and bundle is current. You get LiDAR mapping, a roller mop that lifts on carpet, solid pet hair pickup, and a self-empty/self-wash base. This is the one to look at if you want Roborock-level features without quite paying Roborock money.

Roborock S8 / Qrevo series

Roborock remains the benchmark for navigation and app polish - room-by-room cleaning, no-go zones you can draw with your finger, and genuinely reliable obstacle avoidance. Expect to pay a premium over Ecovacs for a similar spec, often $1,400-$2,200 for the Omni/self-washing flagships. Worth it if you want the smoothest software experience and don't mind paying for it, especially in multi-room homes where mapping accuracy actually gets tested.

Eufy Omni / X-series (X10 Pro Omni, Omni C-series)

Eufy (Anker's home brand) is the sharpest value pick right now. The X10 Pro Omni has been sitting around the $999 mark on NZ price comparison sites, which undercuts most of the competition while still including LiDAR, a self-empty and self-wash base, and hair-tangle-resistant brushes that genuinely reduce the maintenance chore of pulling hair off the roller. Eufy's app is a step behind Roborock's but the core cleaning performance holds up well for the price.

Xiaomi robot vacuums

Xiaomi's models (sold through various NZ importers and occasionally Noel Leeming) tend to undercut the big three on price for similar core specs - LiDAR navigation, reasonable suction, basic mopping. The trade-off is patchier local support and an app that sometimes needs a region workaround. Fine if you're comfortable self-supporting a gadget; less fine if you want someone to call when it won't dock properly.

iRobot Roomba (budget-to-mid Combo and j-series)

Roomba invented the category and Noel Leeming still stocks a solid range, but iRobot has slipped behind Chinese brands on mapping speed and self-empty base reliability at equivalent price points. Still a reasonable choice if you specifically want the most established brand and don't need the newest mopping tech - the entry Combo models are usually the cheapest "real" LiDAR-free option on the shelf.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Buying on suction number alone: a high Pa rating with poor brush design or weak edge-cleaning still leaves crumbs in corners. Read a couple of real reviews, not just the spec sheet.
  • Ignoring your floor plan: lots of thresholds, thick rugs, or a multi-level house punishes cheap navigation systems. Spend more on mapping before you spend more on suction.
  • Forgetting consumables: self-empty bags, mop pads, and replacement filters are an ongoing cost. Check what a pack of dustbags costs before you buy, not after.
  • Expecting it to replace a proper vacuum: robots are brilliant at maintenance cleaning between deeper cleans, not at replacing a stick vacuum for spills, stairs, or car interiors.
Renting or small apartment? A base-model Eufy or Ecovacs without the self-empty dock is usually the smarter buy - you'll empty the bin more often, but you save several hundred dollars on a feature that matters less in a smaller footprint.
