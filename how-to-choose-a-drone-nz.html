How to Choose a Drone in New Zealand

Buying a drone in NZ isn't just about camera specs — the CAA rules shape what you can actually fly and where. Here's a practical framework plus what the law requires.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026
Before you buy: Every drone you fly in NZ, regardless of size, needs an operator ID from AirShare — the free government-linked platform — and there's no getting around the 4km-from-airport rule. Read the CAA section before choosing a model.

Step 1: Decide what you're actually using it for

  • Casual/photo hobbyist: A compact folding drone under 250g, like the DJI Mini 4 Pro, is the sweet spot — light enough to travel with easily and still shoots strong 4K footage with obstacle avoidance.
  • Serious videography/real estate/content creation: Look at heavier drones with larger sensors, like DJI's Air or Mavic series, which trade portability for better low-light image quality and more advanced camera controls.
  • Racing/FPV: A dedicated FPV drone kit with goggles is a different category entirely and usually requires more hands-on flying skill before you're comfortable.

Step 2: Understand the weight threshold that matters

Weight class affects both rules and practicality. Sub-250g drones like the DJI Mini series sit under some of the stricter global weight-based regulations that apply overseas, though New Zealand's CAA Part 101 rules don't currently draw a hard registration line at 250g the way some other countries do — NZ's rules apply broadly regardless of drone weight. Still, lighter drones are easier to carry, less intimidating to fly near people, and cheaper to replace if something goes wrong.

Step 3: Know the CAA rules before you fly (not after)

  • Register with AirShare: New Zealand doesn't require a formal drone registration certificate the way some countries do, but every operator should register on AirShare, the free platform that provides an operator ID, airspace maps, and flight logging.
  • Altitude limit: Standard recreational and commercial flights under Part 101 are capped at 120m above ground level without further authorisation.
  • Line of sight: You must keep your drone within visual line of sight at all times — no flying it out past where you can see it.
  • Airport buffer: Stay at least 4km from any aerodrome, airfield or helipad unless you've got specific authorisation.
  • Wildlife and DOC land: Marine mammal and seal colonies are no-fly zones year-round, and popular DOC tracks (like the Tongariro Alpine Crossing and Milford Track) have restrictions, plus seasonal bird-breeding closures.
  • No licence needed for most flying: Part 101 doesn't distinguish between commercial and recreational use for basic flying, and you can even sell your drone footage without a pilot licence — but you must still follow the same airspace rules.

Check AirShare's interactive map before flying somewhere new — it flags temporary restrictions (like events or emergency response areas) that a generic ruleset won't catch.

Step 4: Compare real NZ pricing before deciding

As a benchmark, the DJI Mini 4 Pro (with the standard RC-N2 controller) sells for around NZ$1,120–$1,320 depending on retailer, while the Fly More Combo bundle (extra batteries, case, and the smarter DJI RC 2 controller with a built-in screen) runs roughly NZ$1,700. NZ retailers stocking DJI drones include PB Tech, JB Hi-Fi, Ferntech and specialist stores like Drone Depot — prices vary enough between them that it's worth comparing before buying.

Step 5: Budget for accessories

Extra batteries (flight time on most consumer drones is 25-34 minutes per battery), a proper carry case, and spare propellers are worth budgeting for from day one — flight time disappears fast once you start actually shooting footage rather than just testing.
