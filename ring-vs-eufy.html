Ring vs Eufy: Which Security Camera Actually Makes Sense

One charges you monthly forever, the other hands storage back to you - here's what that actually costs over three years, and which one we'd put on our own front door.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

The core difference: subscription vs local storage

This is the single biggest thing that separates these two brands, and it should probably drive your decision more than any spec sheet. Ring is built around cloud recording - most Ring cameras and doorbells will show you a live view and send motion alerts for free, but if you want to actually review footage after the fact, you need a Ring Protect subscription. Eufy was built the other way around: most of its cameras and doorbells record locally to the camera itself or a HomeBase hub, so you get full recording, playback and event history with no ongoing fee at all.

Ring

  • Ring Protect Basic: roughly $5-6/month per device (NZD equivalent)
  • Ring Protect Plus: covers every device at one address, roughly $13-15/month
  • Without a plan, most Ring cameras only do live view and basic motion alerts - no video history
  • Deep integration with Amazon Alexa and the wider Amazon smart home ecosystem
  • Very polished app, strong brand recognition, easy setup

Eufy

  • No subscription required for local recording on most models - footage lives on the camera's microSD card or a HomeBase hub
  • Optional Eufy Cloud plan for off-site backup, priced per camera and genuinely optional rather than functionally required
  • Local storage means one-off hardware cost only, which matters a lot for multi-camera setups
  • Works with Google Home and Alexa for basic voice commands, HomeKit on select models, but the ecosystem is thinner than Ring/Amazon's
  • Had a serious privacy scandal in 2023 (unencrypted thumbnails reachable via direct URL) - Eufy says it's been fixed with mandatory encryption since, but it's fair to hold that against them

Running costs over three years

Do the maths on a typical two-camera-plus-doorbell setup and the gap gets hard to ignore. A Ring doorbell plus a Ring Protect Basic plan for three years lands somewhere around $250-350 NZD in subscription fees alone, on top of the hardware. The same setup on Eufy, storing locally, is close to $0 in ongoing fees - you've paid for the hardware and that's it. If you're kitting out a whole house with five or six cameras, Ring's per-device Basic plan add up fast, which is exactly why most multi-camera Ring households end up on the whole-property Plus plan instead.

Video quality and detection

Both brands ship genuinely good hardware in 2026. Ring's newer doorbells and cams do solid 1080p-2K video with reliable person detection, package detection, and now more granular AI alerts (recognising a delivery vs a person lingering vs a vehicle). Eufy's higher-end cameras (the 4G/S-series and its 4K models) match or beat this on resolution and on-device AI person/pet/vehicle recognition, since Eufy leans hard into local AI processing rather than sending clips to the cloud for analysis. Night vision and colour night vision are strong on both; Eufy's spotlight cams tend to have slightly better colour night vision at the price point.

Privacy, in plain terms

Ring's data lives on Amazon's servers, and Amazon has previously faced scrutiny (and a US FTC settlement) over employees having overly broad access to customer video and over sharing user data with police without consent in some cases. Amazon has tightened this up, but "your footage sits on Amazon's cloud by design" is a real consideration for a device that watches your front door.

Eufy's local-first pitch is that your footage never has to leave your house at all - a genuinely stronger privacy position on paper. But the 2023 disclosure that some Eufy doorbell thumbnails and stream data were accessible without proper encryption, before Eufy patched it, is a legitimate mark against a company whose entire brand promise is "your data stays private." Read that as: Eufy's model is more private by design, but its execution record isn't spotless either.

NZ availability and pricing

Both brands are readily available in New Zealand through JB Hi-Fi, Noel Leeming, PB Tech and Mighty Ape. Ring's core doorbell range runs roughly $180-380 NZD depending on model (wired vs battery, standard vs Pro), with cameras from around $150. Eufy's video doorbells sit in a similar $180-400 NZD band, and its camera range spans budget indoor cams from around $80 up to 4K solar-powered outdoor systems well over $500. Neither brand has supply issues here - this isn't a US-only availability problem like some smart home gear.

Mythbusting: "A Ring camera without a subscription is basically useless" isn't quite true anymore - you still get live view, real-time motion push notifications, and two-way talk for free. What you lose without a plan is the ability to scrub back through recorded footage, which is genuinely the main reason you'd want a security camera after an incident. So the myth is close to right for the use case that matters most (proving what happened after the fact), even if the camera "works" in a limited sense without paying.

The verdict

If you want to avoid ongoing costs and you're comfortable managing your own local storage (a microSD card or HomeBase hub), Eufy is the better long-term buy, especially once you're past a single camera. The three-year savings are real money, the hardware is genuinely competitive, and local AI detection is a nice bonus.

If you're already deep in the Amazon/Alexa ecosystem, want the most polished app experience, or you specifically want cloud backup as a hard guarantee against someone stealing the camera itself, Ring is the safer, more mainstream pick - just budget for the subscription from day one rather than treating it as optional.

For most Kiwi households doing a front door plus one or two extra cameras on a budget, we'd lean Eufy. For a full smart-home build already anchored around Alexa, stick with Ring for the smoother integration.
