Continuous Glucose Monitors for Non-Diabetics

CGMs like Abbott Lingo and Stelo are being sold as general wellness wearables, not just diabetes management tools. Here's what they measure, and what's genuinely disputed about using them without a medical need.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

What a CGM actually measures

A continuous glucose monitor is a small sensor, usually worn on the back of the upper arm, that sits just under the skin and measures glucose levels in the interstitial fluid roughly every few minutes. It sends that data to a paired phone app, building a graph of how your blood sugar rises and falls through the day — after meals, exercise, sleep, and stress. This technology has existed for people with diabetes for years, under names like Abbott's FreeStyle Libre and Dexcom's range of sensors. What's changed recently is that companies have launched consumer versions — Abbott Lingo and Dexcom's Stelo among them — explicitly marketed to people without diabetes who want to track metabolic health.

The genuine debate among health professionals

This is a real, unsettled discussion in the medical and dietetics community, not just marketing spin versus reality. Some clinicians and researchers argue that seeing your own glucose response to specific foods can be a useful, personalised motivator for people trying to improve diet and metabolic health, and that data may reveal patterns worth discussing with a doctor. Others point out that normal, healthy glucose fluctuation is just that — normal — and that CGM data interpreted without proper context can cause unnecessary anxiety or lead people without diabetes to make food choices based on incomplete understanding of what their numbers mean. There isn't a settled consensus that CGM use meaningfully improves health outcomes for people who don't have diabetes or prediabetes, and that's worth knowing going in.

This is general information, not medical advice. If you have concerns about your metabolic health, blood sugar, or are considering a CGM for a specific health reason, talk to your GP first. They can advise on whether monitoring is useful for your situation and help you interpret the data properly.

The cost reality

These devices are not a one-off purchase. Sensors typically need replacing every one to two weeks, and consumer CGM programmes are generally sold as an ongoing subscription that bundles the sensors with app access and coaching features. That recurring cost adds up over months, and it's worth working out the true annual cost before committing rather than just looking at the price of a starter pack.

What to weigh up before buying

  • Ongoing sensor cost — budget for continuous replacement, not a single purchase.
  • What the app actually tells you — some platforms offer more coaching and food-logging context than others.
  • Whether you have a specific goal — general curiosity is a weaker reason to commit long-term than a specific health question raised with your doctor.
  • Skin sensitivity — sensor adhesives can cause irritation for some people with continuous wear.

Availability in New Zealand

Consumer CGM programmes aimed at non-diabetics have generally launched in the US and UK first, and availability in New Zealand varies and changes over time — some come through direct-to-consumer online ordering with shipping to NZ, while sensors for diagnosed diabetics are managed through the health system and pharmacies rather than as a retail wellness product. If you're specifically after a wellness-focused CGM programme, check current shipping and support availability for New Zealand before subscribing, since that can shift.

Bottom line

CGMs are legitimate, well-established medical technology, but their value for people without diabetes is genuinely still being debated by health professionals, not a settled fact. If you're curious about metabolic health, start that conversation with your doctor rather than assuming a wearable will give you the full picture on its own.
