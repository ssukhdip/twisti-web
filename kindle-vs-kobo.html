Kindle vs Kobo: Which E-Reader Should You Buy?

Amazon's Kindle and Rakuten's Kobo dominate the e-reader market in New Zealand, and both make genuinely good hardware. The decision usually comes down to where you already buy your ebooks and whether library borrowing matters to you.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

Ecosystem is the real decision

Before comparing screens or battery life, work out where your books will come from. Kindle devices are built around the Amazon ebook store, and while you can sideload other formats, borrowing or buying outside Amazon is clunky by design. Kobo, by contrast, supports the open EPUB format natively and plays nicely with a much wider range of sources.

This matters a lot for New Zealand readers specifically because Kobo has direct, built-in integration with OverDrive/Libby, which many NZ public libraries use for ebook lending. Borrowing a library ebook on a Kobo is usually a few taps from the device itself. On a Kindle, library borrowing is possible but generally involves more steps through the Libby app and your computer.

Library borrowing: Kobo's clear edge

If you're a regular user of your local library's ebook collection, Kobo's native OverDrive support is a genuine practical advantage over Kindle. Kindle users can still borrow library ebooks, but the process typically requires going through the Libby website rather than searching directly on the device.

Hardware and screen tech

Both brands offer a range from entry-level basic readers up through waterproof, warm-light "signature" or "oasis"-tier models with page-turn buttons and larger screens. E-ink panel quality is broadly comparable between equivalent tiers of each brand — both use similar generation E Ink Carta displays, so don't expect a dramatic difference in screen sharpness between similarly priced models.

  • Entry-level models: Both brands offer a basic, non-waterproof option at the lower end of the price range, good for straightforward reading.
  • Mid-range: Waterproofing and adjustable warm lighting typically appear from the mid-tier upward on both brands.
  • Premium: Larger screens, page-turn buttons and stylus support (for note-taking models) sit at the top of each lineup, at a noticeably higher price.

File format and flexibility

Kobo supports EPUB natively, which is the most common format for ebooks purchased outside the big retailers, including many independent and NZ-based ebook stores. Kindle uses its own format and needs conversion tools for EPUB files, though Amazon has improved compatibility over the years. If you read a lot of library books, review copies, or ebooks from smaller retailers, Kobo tends to be less hassle day to day.

Note: If you're already deep into Amazon's ecosystem — Prime, Audible, an existing Kindle library — switching to Kobo means leaving purchased Kindle books behind unless you strip DRM, which is a hassle most people won't bother with. Sticking with Kindle is often the practical choice for existing Amazon customers.

Price and where to buy in NZ

Both brands are sold through major NZ electronics retailers as well as directly, and pricing tends to shift around sales periods. Entry models generally sit at a more accessible price point, while premium waterproof or note-taking models command a significant premium. It's worth comparing current prices across retailers rather than assuming one brand is always cheaper — promotional pricing varies a lot throughout the year.

Battery life is roughly comparable across both ecosystems at similar tiers, typically lasting weeks rather than days under normal reading habits, though this drops if you keep wireless connectivity on constantly.
