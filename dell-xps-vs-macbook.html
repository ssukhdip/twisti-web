Dell XPS vs MacBook

Windows' best-built laptop against Apple's own — the closest fight in this whole series. Here's who actually wins, and for whom.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

Windows' best-built laptop against Apple's default

Dell briefly rebranded its premium line as 'Dell Premium' and then walked it back — XPS is the name again, and the 2026 lineup (XPS 13 and XPS 14, with the XPS 16 rounding out the range) is arguably the strongest XPS generation yet. It's the laptop most often held up as 'the Windows MacBook' — CNC-machined aluminium, minimalist design, OLED options — so this comparison is closer than most Windows-vs-Mac match-ups.

The XPS 14 (2026) starts at roughly US$1,599 (expect NZ pricing to land somewhere in the $2,700-$3,000+ range once converted and taxed, scaling up toward US$4,000 for the loaded configuration), running an Intel Core Ultra 5 325 at the base with 16GB RAM and a 2K display, up to a Core Ultra X9 388H, 64GB RAM, 2TB SSD and a 2.8K (2880x1800) OLED touch InfinityEdge display at the top. The MacBook Air starts at $1,699-$1,957 NZD, with the MacBook Pro (M4/M4 Pro/M4 Max) starting around $2,999 NZD and climbing well past $5,000 for Max configurations.

Build and design

Dell XPS 14

1.36kg, CNC-machined aluminium chassis, a genuinely excellent keyboard with a full function row (Dell reversed the capacitive-row misstep of earlier XPS generations), a large haptic trackpad, and that InfinityEdge OLED display on higher configs. It looks and feels like a laptop built to compete directly with Apple, because it was.

MacBook Air / Pro

Still the reference point for unibody aluminium construction. The Air is 1.24kg and fanless; the Pro adds a fan, a brighter mini-LED display on 14/16-inch models, and noticeably more connectivity (SD card slot, HDMI, MagSafe). Both feel inseparably solid — this is genuinely a coin-flip category now.

Display

The XPS 14's top OLED configuration (2880x1800, HDR-capable touch panel) is arguably the more spectacular screen on paper — OLED contrast beats any LCD, including the MacBook Air's Liquid Retina panel. The MacBook Pro's mini-LED display closes most of that gap with excellent HDR and a 120Hz ProMotion refresh the XPS doesn't match. If you want touch and OLED punch, Dell wins; if you want the smoothest scrolling and best sustained brightness, the MacBook Pro wins. The MacBook Air's basic LCD is the weakest screen of the three, though still perfectly good for everyday use.

Performance

Intel's Core Ultra Series 3 ('Panther Lake') chips in the XPS 14 are a genuine step up for Intel — built on the 18A node with a strong Arc B390 integrated GPU (up to 12 Xe3 cores), competitive single-threaded performance, and much-improved efficiency versus previous Intel generations. Apple's M4 (and M4 Pro/Max in the Pro line) still leads on sustained multi-core efficiency and thermal headroom — the MacBook Pro can sustain high performance for far longer under heavy export or render loads than most thin Windows laptops, XPS included, without throttling or spinning up loud fans.

Battery life

This remains Apple's clearest win. The MacBook Air routinely delivers 15-18 hours of real mixed use; the MacBook Pro isn't far behind despite its extra power. The XPS 14, even with Intel's efficiency gains, typically lands in the 10-13 hour range in real-world testing — very good for a Windows laptop, still a meaningful gap versus either Mac.

Ports and repairability

  • XPS 14: USB-C/Thunderbolt ports, though Dell has trimmed legacy ports in the name of thinness — you'll likely need a dongle for USB-A or HDMI on some configurations, same complaint people have long had about the MacBook Air.
  • MacBook Pro: actually more generous here — HDMI, SD card slot, and MagSafe alongside Thunderbolt, a genuine practical advantage for creatives who plug in cameras and monitors regularly.
Myth worth killing: 'Dell XPS is just a MacBook clone that's worse.' It isn't — the current XPS 14's OLED touch display, function-row keyboard fix, and Windows software compatibility make it a genuinely competitive alternative, not a knockoff. The real trade-off is battery life and thermal sustain under heavy load, not overall quality.

Verdict

Buy the Dell XPS if: you need Windows software or PC gaming access, you want a touchscreen with OLED punch, and you can live with roughly 3-5 fewer hours of battery than a comparable MacBook.

Buy the MacBook Air if: you want the best battery life and quietest everyday laptop at the lowest entry price, and you don't need Windows-specific software.

Buy the MacBook Pro if: you do sustained creative work — video export, 3D rendering, large compiles — where thermal headroom and native Apple Silicon app support (Final Cut, Logic, Adobe) actually save you real time.

Our honest take: this is the closest premium-laptop comparison on the market right now. If you're Windows-committed, the XPS 14 is the best Dell has made in years and a legitimately good buy. If you're platform-agnostic, the Mac's battery life and thermal consistency still tip it for most people.
