Best Drawing Tablet NZ: Which One to Buy

A drawing tablet is a genuinely different purchase depending on whether you're a curious beginner or a working illustrator. Here's what separates the two, and what specs are worth paying for.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

Pen-only tablets vs screen tablets

A pen-only (non-display) tablet like a Wacom Intuos connects to your computer and you draw on the tablet surface while watching the screen — this involves a hand-eye coordination adjustment period but is far cheaper and still the standard entry point for learning digital art. A screen tablet (Wacom Cintiq-style, or standalone devices) lets you draw directly on the screen where the art appears, which feels far more natural immediately but costs significantly more. Beginners often assume they need a screen tablet to get started; most professional illustrators actually learned and still work comfortably on pen-only tablets.

What a genuine beginner actually needs

A budget pen-only tablet with a reasonable active area (roughly A5-ish) and at least 4096 levels of pressure sensitivity covers everything a beginner needs to learn digital drawing fundamentals, well before screen tablets or professional pen displays become worth the extra cost. Don't let "the pros use a Cintiq" talk you into overspending before you know if digital art is something you'll stick with.

Pressure sensitivity — where the number stops mattering

Pressure sensitivity is measured in levels (2048, 4096, 8192), but past roughly 4096 levels, the practical difference is barely perceptible to the human hand — this spec becomes a marketing number game beyond that point. What matters more in practice is pressure curve consistency (does light pressure feel smooth and predictable across the whole tablet surface) and tilt sensitivity if you want natural shading effects, which higher-end pens support and cheaper ones often skip.

Standalone tablets (no computer needed)

Some newer tablets run as standalone Android-based devices with drawing apps built in, letting you draw without connecting to a PC at all. These suit people who want portability and don't need professional software like Photoshop or Clip Studio Paint plugged into a full desktop workflow — but they're generally less powerful for serious illustration work than a tablet connected to a proper computer.

Who should actually spend more

  • Complete beginners testing the waters: start with a budget pen-only tablet — no need to spend big before knowing if you'll stick with digital art.
  • Hobbyists who've committed to it: a mid-range pen-only tablet with a larger active area and tilt support is a worthwhile step up.
  • Working illustrators and professionals: a screen tablet (Cintiq-class or equivalent) genuinely speeds up workflow and reduces the disconnect between hand and eye — worth the investment when it's your income.
  • Students in design/art courses: check what your specific course or software requires before buying — some schools have minimum spec or brand expectations for compatibility with lab software.
Honest take: most people who buy an expensive screen tablet as their first drawing device end up using a fraction of its capability while still learning the fundamentals. Start cheaper, upgrade once you know your own habits and needs.
