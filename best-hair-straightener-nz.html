Best Hair Straightener NZ: Which to Buy

GHD-style straighteners can cost four times what a supermarket one does. Whether that's worth it depends heavily on your hair type and how often you actually use it.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

Ceramic vs titanium plates

Ceramic plates heat more evenly and gently, which makes them more forgiving on fine, thin, or already-damaged hair — less risk of hot spots that scorch a section of hair while the rest is still cool. Titanium plates heat up faster and hold a higher, more consistent temperature throughout a styling session, gliding more smoothly through thick, coarse, or curly hair that takes more heat to actually straighten. Many mid-to-premium straighteners now use a ceramic-coated titanium or tourmaline-ceramic blend, aiming for the even heat of ceramic with the speed and durability of titanium.

  • Fine, fragile, colour-treated hair: pure ceramic plates at a lower temperature setting are the safer choice.
  • Thick, coarse, or very curly hair: titanium or ceramic-titanium plates handle the higher heat needed more evenly.
  • Plate width matters too: narrower plates (25-30mm) suit short or fine hair and give more control near the roots; wider plates (38mm+) cover more hair per pass and suit long or thick hair.

Heat damage is real — what temperature actually means

Hair damage from styling tools is cumulative and genuinely irreversible once it happens — split ends and structural damage from heat don't heal, they only grow out. As a rough, widely-cited guide from hairdressers: fine or damaged hair should stay under 160°C, normal hair is generally fine up to 180-190°C, and only thick or coarse, undamaged hair should go above 200°C. Higher heat straightens faster, but that's not the same as "better" — going in at your hair's minimum effective temperature and doing a couple of slower passes causes less damage than blasting through at maximum heat in one fast pass.

Honest take: a precise digital temperature dial is worth having specifically so you can dial down to what your hair actually needs, rather than a straightener that only offers "low/medium/high" with no real numbers, or worse, no adjustment at all.

Is a premium (GHD-style) straightener worth it over budget?

The genuine differences at the premium end are: more even and more accurate heat (less risk of hot spots even at high settings), better long-term durability of the heating element and hinge under daily use, and often a auto-shutoff safety feature. If you straighten your hair most days, that durability and heat consistency adds up over a couple of years and is a reasonable case for spending more.

If you straighten your hair occasionally — a few times a month for an event — a mid-range ceramic straightener from a known brand will do the job well and the premium price difference is much harder to justify. The marketing halo around specific premium brands is real, but the plates and heating tech in solid mid-range straighteners from brands like Remington or BaByliss have genuinely improved in recent years.

Where to buy one in NZ

Farmers and Chemist Warehouse carry the widest range across budget to premium including GHD, ghd's main direct competitor. Noel Leeming and Harvey Norman stock mid-range Remington, BaByliss and Philips models well, often with better bundle deals than pharmacy stores. PB Tech is worth a check for occasional clearance pricing on well-known brands.
