BUYING GUIDE

Best Food Processor NZ: Which to Buy

Most food processors ship with six attachments and owners use two of them. Here's which features are actually worth paying for and which just add to the box of unused discs in your cupboard.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

Bowl capacity: match it to how you actually cook

A compact 2-3L bowl food processor is genuinely enough for most everyday tasks — chopping onions, making a pesto, blitzing breadcrumbs, grating a block of cheese. Larger 3.5-4.5L bowls make sense if you regularly cook in bulk, make big batches of dough, or cook for a large household. Buying the biggest bowl available just to be safe usually just means more bench space taken up and a heavier bowl to wash for tasks that never needed the extra capacity in the first place.

Attachments: which ones actually get used

The standard S-blade for chopping and the slicing/grating discs are the attachments that see genuine regular use in most kitchens — grating a block of cheese or slicing a pile of vegetables by hand is exactly the tedious task a food processor should be saving you time on. Dough blades, julienne discs, whisking attachments and citrus juicers included in bigger sets tend to sit unused after the first month for most home cooks, because a stand mixer does dough better and a dedicated juicer does citrus better. Before paying extra for a bigger attachment set, be honest about which of those you'd actually reach for regularly.

Food processor vs blender vs stand mixer: the real overlap

Food processor: best for chopping, slicing, grating, shredding, and pastry/dough work — dry and semi-dry tasks.

Blender: best for liquids — smoothies, soups, sauces — anything that needs a fully smooth, pourable result.

Stand mixer: best for baking-specific tasks — whipping cream, kneading bread dough at volume, creaming butter and sugar.

Motor durability for tough tasks like dough

Kneading bread dough is one of the toughest jobs you can ask a food processor motor to do, and it's where cheap units genuinely fail or overheat — if bread and pizza dough are a regular part of your cooking, this is the single task worth researching motor specs and reviews for specifically, more than any other feature. Mid-range and above processors from Breville, KitchenAid and Kenwood tend to handle dough loads that budget models can't sustain without straining or tripping a thermal cutoff.

Honest overlap check: If you already own a decent blender and a stand mixer, a food processor's unique value is really just the chopping/slicing/grating tasks — worth confirming you actually do those often enough by hand currently to justify the extra appliance and bench space.

What to check before buying

  • Bowl capacity matched to real usage — not the biggest one available just in case.
  • Which attachments you'll genuinely use — don't pay extra for a large attachment set you won't touch.
  • Motor rating if dough is a regular task — this is where budget units fail first.
  • Feed tube size — a wider feed tube means less pre-chopping of vegetables before processing.
  • Storage for attachments — some models include a dedicated case, which matters more than it sounds once you own six discs and blades.

Bottom line

Buy a food processor for chopping, slicing, grating and dough — not as a blender or stand mixer replacement, since it genuinely doesn't do either of those jobs as well. Match bowl size to your real household cooking volume and be realistic about which attachments you'll actually use before paying for a bigger set.
