Best White Noise Machine NZ: Which One to Buy

A dedicated white noise machine is a small, single-purpose speaker that loops ambient sound all night — the question is whether that's worth buying over a free app or a smart speaker you already own.

Twisti guides · Updated August 2026

Dedicated machine vs phone app vs smart speaker

A phone app is free and works immediately, but it ties up your phone overnight (charging next to your head, notifications interrupting playback) and phone speakers are usually thin and tinny at the low frequencies that actually mask disruptive noise. A smart speaker you already own is a genuinely good free option if it has reasonable bass response. A dedicated white noise machine's real advantage is a speaker actually tuned for broadband noise, a physical volume dial you can adjust half-asleep, and no phone/screen anywhere near the bed.

The looping-glitch problem

Cheaper white noise machines use a short looped sample rather than genuinely random noise generation, and on the cheapest models that loop point is audible as a faint repeating "whoosh" every few seconds — something your brain can pick up on and find more disruptive than the noise is meant to prevent. Better machines use longer loops or true noise generation so there's no audible seam. This is genuinely the biggest quality difference between a $20 machine and a $60 one, more than raw volume or sound options.

Sound types that actually matter

White noise (flat, hissy) is the most broadly effective at masking speech and sudden sounds. Pink noise (deeper, more like rainfall) is what most sleep research actually points to as more pleasant and equally effective for many people. Brown noise (deeper still, like a low rumble) suits people masking traffic or a snoring partner. Nature sounds (rain, ocean, fan) are pleasant but usually less effective at genuinely masking other noise than true white/pink/brown noise — worth knowing if the goal is function over ambience.

Genuine use cases

  • Baby sleep: the most common reason people buy one — a consistent, non-changing sound genuinely helps some babies (and parents trying not to wake them) more than a phone app that might buzz with a notification.
  • Tinnitus masking: many audiologists recommend broadband noise at night specifically because tinnitus is often most noticeable in silence — a dedicated machine avoids screen light and phone dependency at 2am.
  • Thin-walled apartments/shared houses: masking a neighbour's TV or a flatmate's late arrival home.
  • Travel: a small battery/USB-powered unit for unfamiliar hotel-room noise.
Auto-off timers: If you only want noise while falling asleep rather than all night, check the timer options before buying — some budget models only offer all-night or no-timer at all, with no in-between setting.

Volume and safety for babies

If buying specifically for a nursery, keep the machine well away from the cot (not right next to baby's head) and avoid the maximum volume setting — health guidance generally recommends white noise for infants stays at a moderate volume and reasonable distance, similar to a quiet shower running in another room, not loud continuous exposure right next to the crib.
