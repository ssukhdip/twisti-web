Best TV Under $2000 (NZ, 2026)

Under $2000 is where mini-LED QLED and entry OLED start genuinely competing - here is how to pick between them honestly, with real NZ models and pricing.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

What actually matters at this price

Once you're spending up to $2000 in NZ, you're no longer just buying "a 4K TV" — you're choosing between meaningfully different technologies, and the right call depends on your room and habits more than the spec sheet. Here's the honest breakdown.

Panel type: this is the bracket where mid-range QLED, mini-LED, and entry-level OLED all genuinely compete. QLED with mini-LED backlighting (smaller, more precisely controlled zones of light) gets you noticeably better contrast and brightness control than basic LED-QLED. Entry OLED (LG's B-series or similar, and some Samsung/Sony QD-OLED at 55 inch on sale) starts to appear at the top of this range — OLED's individually lit pixels mean true blacks and no backlight blooming, which matters a lot for movies in a dark room, but OLED panels are typically dimmer in a bright room and carry a small risk of burn-in with static content (news tickers, game HUDs left on pause for hours).

Resolution and refresh rate: 4K is standard here too. Where this bracket differs from sub-$1000 is that native 120Hz panels genuinely start showing up, particularly on TVs marketed with gaming features (HDMI 2.1, VRR, ALLM). If you've got a PS5 or Xbox Series X and actually play fast-paced games, this is the bracket where 120Hz starts being worth paying for. If you mostly watch TV and stream, it's still a nice-to-have rather than essential.

HDR: Dolby Vision plus HDR10+ support becomes common at this level, and — more importantly — the TVs are bright enough to actually make HDR content look the way it's meant to. This is where HDR stops being a checkbox and starts being a visible upgrade over the $1000 bracket.

Smart TV OS: Google TV (TCL, Sony, Hisense in places), Tizen (Samsung) and webOS (LG) are all mature at this point. Differences come down to personal preference on layout and how pushy the home screen ads are (Samsung and LG have both been criticised for this in recent years) — worth a quick look at reviews if that bothers you.

Sound: better than sub-$1000 sets, especially models with a few more drivers or basic upward-firing speakers, but still nowhere near what a $150 soundbar delivers. At this spend level we'd genuinely suggest budgeting for at least an entry soundbar if you care about movie or game sound — see below.

Our picks under $2000

Hisense U7 series mini-LED (65 inch, roughly $1,500 to $1,800)

The U7 is the mini-LED sweet spot in this bracket — genuinely bright, punchy HDR, and a 65 inch size that would cost considerably more from Samsung or LG for similar backlight tech. VIDAA's smart platform is functional if a little less refined than Google TV, but the picture quality per dollar here is hard to beat. Best for someone prioritising a big, vivid screen for movies and sport over premium software polish.

Samsung Q60 / Q70 series QLED (55 to 65 inch, roughly $1,000 to $1,800 depending on size and sale)

Samsung's mid-range QLED line is the safe, well-supported choice — Tizen is slick, the Samsung remote and app ecosystem is convenient if you already own Samsung or Galaxy gear, and picture quality is solid without mini-LED backlighting. Suits buyers who want a recognisable, reliable brand and don't need the absolute best contrast on the market.

LG QNED86 / mini-LED series (55 to 65 inch, roughly $1,200 to $2,000)

LG's mini-LED QNED range sits between Samsung's Q60/Q70 and full OLED — better local dimming than basic QLED, webOS is one of the nicer smart interfaces around, and LG remotes remain a highlight. A good middle-ground pick for someone who wants better-than-entry picture quality without stretching to OLED.

Sony Bravia entry/mid LED-LCD (55 inch, roughly $1,300 to $1,900)

Sony's image processing has long been a strength even on non-flagship models — motion handling and colour accuracy tend to look a notch more natural than the spec sheet alone would suggest, and Google TV runs well on Sony hardware. This is the pick for someone who watches a lot of streaming content and cares more about consistently accurate colour than maximum brightness or gaming features.

Entry OLED, 55 inch (LG B-series class or equivalent Samsung/Sony QD-OLED on sale, roughly $1,700 to $2,000)

If you can stretch to it and mostly watch in a dim or moderately lit room, entry OLED at 55 inch is the biggest genuine quality jump in this guide — true blacks, no blooming around bright objects, and excellent contrast for movies. Not the pick for a bright, sunny lounge, and be mindful of leaving static images (paused games, news channels) on screen for extended periods.

Size versus panel trade-off: at this budget you can typically get a bigger mini-LED QLED or a smaller OLED — rarely both. If movie night in a dark room is the priority, lean OLED at 55 inch. If it's sport, family viewing, or a bright living room, lean bigger mini-LED QLED at 65 inch.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Oversized "was" price gimmicks: this bracket sees some of the most aggressive "was $3499, now $1799" style pricing around sale events. Check PriceSpy or similar and the retailer's own price history where possible — the discount is sometimes real, but sometimes the "was" price only existed for a week before the "sale" began.
  • Chasing 120Hz you don't need: genuinely useful if you game on current-gen consoles or PC; largely irrelevant if you don't. Don't let it push you toward a worse all-round TV just to tick this box.
  • Refresh rate marketing terms: at this price, check for native panel refresh rate (60Hz or 120Hz) specifically, plus HDMI 2.1 if gaming matters — ignore made-up "effective" motion numbers that vary by brand and aren't comparable across manufacturers.
  • Assuming a soundbar is optional: at this spend level, more than sub-$1000, we'd say factor one in. Even a $150 to $250 basic soundbar noticeably improves dialogue clarity and bass over any built-in TV speaker at this size, and it's a much smaller investment than jumping to the next TV tier for marginally better sound.
  • Buying OLED for a bright room: OLED's dark-room strengths become a weakness with lots of daylight or bright ceiling lights hitting the screen — a mini-LED QLED will actually look better in that environment despite being the "lower tier" tech on paper.

The honest takeaway: under $2000 in NZ, you're choosing a trade-off, not a single "best" TV. Bigger and brighter mini-LED QLED, or smaller and deeper-contrast OLED — both are legitimate answers depending on your room and how you actually watch.
