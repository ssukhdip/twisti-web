Air Fryer vs Oven: What They Actually Cost to Run in NZ

Everyone says air fryers are cheaper to run than an oven, but almost nobody shows the actual maths. We did it using real NZ power rates and real appliance wattages, meal by meal.

Twisti guides · Updated August 2026

Why the comparison isn't as simple as wattage on the box

It's tempting to look at an air fryer rated 1500W and an oven rated 2200W and assume the air fryer is automatically cheaper. That's only half the story. What actually determines your power bill is energy used, which is wattage multiplied by the time the element is drawing that power — measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh). An oven pulls more watts, but a lot of that draw happens during preheat, and ovens cycle their element on and off to hold temperature rather than running flat out the whole time. An air fryer's small chamber means it reaches temperature almost immediately and cooks faster, but small food volumes and multiple batches change the equation quickly.

For this comparison we used a typical 2026 New Zealand residential electricity rate of $0.32 per kWh (uncontrolled, non-time-of-use plans commonly sit between $0.30 and $0.35/kWh depending on lines company and retailer — Contact, Mercury, Genesis and Frank Energy all land in roughly that band for the North Island and upper South Island).

The actual numbers: cooking a tray of chips

Frozen chips, one standard 750g bag

Air fryer (Philips Essential XL, 1750W): 18 minutes at full draw, no preheat needed. Energy used: 1.75kW × 0.3 hours = 0.525kWh. Cost: ≈17c.

Conventional oven (freestanding 2300W element, average draw once cycling accounted for is roughly 60% of rated wattage): 8 minutes preheat to 220°C plus 25 minutes cook = 33 minutes at an effective ~1.4kW average draw. Energy used: 1.4kW × 0.55 hours = 0.77kWh. Cost: ≈25c.

On this single tray, the air fryer wins by about 8 cents. That sounds trivial, but 8 cents per meal, cooked most nights of the week, adds up to roughly $25–30 a year for one dish alone — and chips are far from the biggest gap.

Where the air fryer really pulls ahead: small, fast meals

The savings get much larger for meals an oven is inefficient at cooking, because you're forced to run a large oven cavity to heat a small amount of food. Reheating four chicken nuggets or crisping bacon for two people are the clearest cases:

  • Reheating leftovers (10 min): air fryer ≈ 8c vs oven (15 min preheat + 10 min cook) ≈ 20c.
  • Bacon and hash browns for two (14 min): air fryer ≈ 13c vs oven (preheat-heavy, 30 min total) ≈ 26c.
  • Roast chicken thighs, 6 pieces (25 min): air fryer ≈ 24c vs oven (40 min total including preheat) ≈ 34c — a smaller gap because the oven's larger capacity starts to offset its inefficiency.

Where the oven claws it back: batch cooking

This is the tradeoff most air fryer marketing conveniently skips. A standard basket-style air fryer (the Philips XL and similar 6.2–7.3L models included) genuinely struggles past 3–4 servings without cooking in batches, and each extra batch adds its own near-full cook time — there's minimal efficiency gain from "it's already hot" the way an oven benefits from staying at temperature. Cook a full roast dinner for a family of five — a tray of vegetables, a protein, and a side — and a single 45-minute oven bake comfortably undercuts three sequential air fryer batches on both cost and, frankly, your patience.

Real number to remember: the crossover point is roughly 3 batches. Below that, an air fryer is normally cheaper and faster. At 3 or more sequential batches to feed a household, a single oven bake starts costing less overall and takes less hands-on time, even though its instantaneous wattage is higher.

Quality tradeoffs that matter more than the cents

Cost isn't the only reason people switch, and it shouldn't be the only reason you decide. Air fryers use rapid convection in a small chamber, which genuinely produces crispier results on things like fries, wings and reheated pizza than a conventional oven — the airflow is more concentrated and the food sits closer to the heat source. Ovens still win for even browning across large or irregularly shaped items (a whole chicken, a tray of roast vegetables that need to caramelise evenly) and for anything that needs gentle, low-temperature cooking like slow-roasted lamb shoulder, where an air fryer's aggressive fan can dry food out.

Basket capacity is the other practical limiter. Most sub-$200 NZ air fryers (Ninja, Philips, Kmart's own-brand, Russell Hobbs) top out around 5.5–7.3 litres, which is genuinely tight for more than 2–3 people per batch. If you regularly cook for a family of four or more in a single sitting, factor batching time into any cost comparison, not just the electricity.

Standby and idle costs

Neither appliance draws meaningful power on standby — ovens with digital clocks pull a negligible trickle (well under $2/year), and most air fryers have no standby draw at all once unplugged from the wall at the switch, which is worth doing since many NZ power boards leave appliances live at the wall.

Bottom line

For everyday meals — reheats, small proteins, chips, roast veg for one or two — an air fryer will save you real money in New Zealand, typically 30–50% per meal compared to a conventional oven at current power prices, plus it's faster and doesn't heat up your kitchen in summer. But if you regularly cook full meals for four or more people in one sitting, the oven's larger single-batch capacity means it can end up cheaper and less hassle than three or four consecutive air fryer batches. Most NZ households get the best of both: air fryer for solo and weeknight cooking, oven for family roasts and big batches.
