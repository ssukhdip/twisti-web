Best Mini PC NZ: Which One to Buy

Mini PCs have quietly gotten good enough to replace a tower for most people. Here's who actually benefits, what specs matter, and where a bigger machine still wins.

Twisti guides · Updated July 2026

What counts as a "mini PC" in 2026

The category covers a few different things now. There's Apple's Mac mini, the small NUC-style Windows boxes from Intel's successors and ASUS, and the cheaper Chinese-made options from Beelink and GMKtec that have flooded the market over the last couple of years. They're all roughly the size of a paperback book, sit under or behind a monitor, and draw a fraction of the power a tower does. The gap between them is mostly in the chip inside — some run mobile laptop processors repackaged into a desktop shell, which is fine for most tasks but won't match a proper desktop CPU under sustained load.

Who actually needs one

If your daily use is browsers, spreadsheets, video calls, streaming, and general home-office work, a mini PC is genuinely enough — and arguably better, since it's quiet, small, and cheap to run continuously. They also make excellent media centre boxes plugged into a TV, and a growing number of people are using them as always-on home servers for things like Plex, Home Assistant, or a NAS front-end. Light gaming is workable too, particularly on the Mac mini with Apple Silicon or higher-end mini PCs with a half-decent integrated GPU, though don't expect to run demanding modern titles at high settings.

Mini PC vs tower — the honest trade-off

A mini PC wins on space, noise, and power draw. A tower wins on upgradability, sustained performance, and dedicated graphics power. If you're doing serious gaming, video editing, 3D rendering, or anything that needs a discrete GPU and years of upgrade headroom, a tower or a proper desktop replacement laptop is still the better buy. Mini PCs are largely sealed units — what you buy is close to what you're stuck with.

RAM and storage: don't underspec it

This is where people get caught out, because many mini PCs solder the RAM to the board, meaning it can't be upgraded later. A few things worth locking in before you buy:

  • 16GB RAM minimum for general use in 2026 — 8GB feels tight once you've got a browser with twenty tabs open alongside anything else.
  • Check if RAM is soldered or in SO-DIMM slots — some Beelink and GMKtec models still use replaceable memory, which gives you a path to upgrade later.
  • 512GB SSD as a baseline, more if you're storing media locally rather than in the cloud.
  • Confirm the number and type of USB ports and video outputs — some budget models skimp on ports to hit a price point.
  • Look at Wi-Fi and Ethernet specs — a mini PC used as a home server benefits a lot from Gigabit Ethernet rather than relying on Wi-Fi.

NZ pricing context

Mini PCs sit across a wide price band in New Zealand. The Mac mini starts at the premium end and holds its value well, while Windows-based options from Beelink and GMKtec are usually cheaper and sold through smaller importers as well as some larger retailers, with pricing that moves around often. You'll also see NUC-style machines and small-form-factor Windows boxes at PB Tech, Noel Leeming, JB Hi-Fi, and Harvey Norman, generally at a premium over the budget import brands but with local warranty support that's easier to action if something goes wrong.

Worth knowing: budget mini PC brands change specs and suppliers frequently, so the exact same model name can ship with different chips over time. Always check the specific processor and RAM configuration listed on the product page you're buying from, not just the marketing name.

Bottom line

For most home and office users, a mini PC is a smart, space-saving upgrade that will comfortably handle daily computing and general home-server duties. If your workload leans into gaming, editing, or anything GPU-heavy, put the money into a tower instead.
